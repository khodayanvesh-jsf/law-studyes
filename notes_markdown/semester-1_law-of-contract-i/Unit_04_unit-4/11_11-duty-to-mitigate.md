# 11. Duty to mitigate
**Subject:** Law of Contract – I (Semester 1) | **Unit 4:** Unit 4
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-i/duty-to-mitigate/

## In One Minute
When a contract is broken, the injured party must act sensibly. He must take reasonable steps to keep his loss small . He cannot sit still, let the loss grow, and then bill the wrongdoer for all of it. Loss that reasonable steps would have avoided is not paid . The rule comes from the Explanation to Section 73 : in estimating the loss, "the means which existed of remedying the inconvenience" must be taken into account. It is a limit on what he can claim , not a duty anyone can sue him for. Reasonable costs of mitigating are recoverable, and gains made by mitigating are deducted. In exams, the meaning, the rules (reasonable steps, nothing extraordinary, costs, gains, burden of proof) and the cases ( Murlidhar Chiranjilal , Jamal , British Westinghouse , Payzu ) earn the marks. In real life, the rule decides how much of a large loss a court will actually award.

## Legal Provision
- Section 73 (first two paragraphs). When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach of it. Such compensation is not to be given for any remote and indirect loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach.
- Explanation. In estimating the loss or damage arising from a breach of contract, the means which existed of remedying the inconvenience caused by the non-performance of the contract must be taken into account.
- Illustration (a). A contracts to sell and deliver 50 maunds of saltpetre to B, at a certain price to be paid on delivery. A breaks his promise. B is entitled to receive from A, by way of compensation, the sum, if any, by which the contract price falls short of the price for which B might have obtained 50 maunds of saltpetre of like quality at the time when the saltpetre ought to have been delivered.
- Illustration (b). A hires B's ship to go to Bombay, and there take on board, on the first of January, a cargo, which A is to provide, and to bring it to Calcutta, the freight to be paid when earned. B's ship does not go to Bombay, but A has opportunities of procuring suitable conveyance for the cargo upon terms as advantageous as those on which he had chartered the ship. A avails himself of those opportunities, but is put to trouble and expense in doing so. A is entitled to receive compensation from B in respect of such trouble and expense.
- In simple words
- Section 73 pays for loss that arises naturally from the breach, and for loss both sides knew was likely. It does not pay for remote loss. The Explanation says: count what the injured party could have done to reduce the loss. Illustration (a) measures the loss by the price at which the buyer might have bought elsewhere, so he is expected to buy elsewhere. Illustration (b) shows the other side: the reasonable trouble and expense of mitigating is paid by the wrongdoer. Illustrations (c), (d), (g) and (h) also measure loss by the market price, which shows what the injured party could have got elsewhere.

## Meaning and Definition
Mitigation means taking reasonable steps to reduce the loss caused by a breach.

The Supreme Court stated the rule in Murlidhar Chiranjilal v. Harishchandra Dwarkadas (1961). It said there are two settled principles under Section 73 read with its Explanation:

the injured party is to be placed, as far as money can do it, in as good a situation as if the contract had been performed; but he has a duty to take all reasonable steps to mitigate the loss, and he cannot claim any part of the damage that is due to his neglect to take such steps.

The Privy Council said the same earlier in Jamal v. Moolla Dawood, Sons & Co. (1915).

Why the law has this rule. Fairness. The wrongdoer should pay for the loss his breach caused, not for the part the injured party could easily have avoided.

It is not a true "duty". The injured party is not liable to anyone for failing to mitigate. The wrongdoer cannot sue him. He only loses the right to recover the avoidable part of the loss.

## Key Points to Remember
- The injured party must take reasonable steps to reduce his loss (Explanation to Section 73). Avoidable loss cannot be recovered. He need not take extraordinary or risky steps, or accept a lower-status job. The reasonable cost of mitigation is recoverable (illustration (b)). A gain made by mitigating is set off against the damages. The wrongdoer must prove the failure to mitigate. For goods, the loss is fixed at the date of breach . Delay is at the injured party's risk. Failure to mitigate only reduces the damages. No one can sue for it.
