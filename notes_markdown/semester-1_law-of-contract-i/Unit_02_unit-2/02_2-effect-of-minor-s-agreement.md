# 2. Effect of Minor's Agreement
**Subject:** Law of Contract – I (Semester 1) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-i/minors-agreements/

## In One Minute
A minor 's agreement is void from the very beginning ( void ab initio ). It is not merely voidable . The minor cannot be sued on it, cannot be made to pay damages for breaking it, and cannot make it valid later by ratification . The Privy Council settled this in Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903). The law still softens the rule in a few places: A supplier of necessaries is paid from the minor's property (Section 68). A minor may receive a benefit , for example as a mortgagee who has paid the money. A court may ask a minor to give back a benefit he still holds (Specific Relief Act, 1963, Section 33(2)(b)). A guardian may, within his powers and for the minor's benefit, make a contract that binds the minor. In exams, this is a favourite problem question. Marks come from Mohori Bibee , the "no ratification, no estoppel" points, restitution, necessaries, and the minor as beneficiary , partner and agent. In real life, it tells a lender or a shop what it can and cannot recover after dealing with someone under 18.

## Legal Provision
- Section 2(g). An agreement not enforceable by law is said to be void;
- Section 10 (first paragraph). All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void.
- Section 11. Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority according to the law to which he is subject, and who is of sound mind, and is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject.
- Section 68. Claim for necessaries supplied to person incapable of contracting, or on his account. If a person, incapable of entering into a contract, or any one whom he is legally bound to support, is supplied by another person with necessaries suited to his condition in life, the person who has furnished such supplies is entitled to be reimbursed from the property of such incapable person.
- Section 184. Who may be an agent. As between the principal and third persons, any person may become an agent, but no person who is not of the age of majority and of sound mind can become an agent, so as to be responsible to his principal according to the provisions in that behalf herein contained.
- Specific Relief Act, 1963, Section 33(2). Where a defendant successfully resists any suit on the ground— … (b) that the agreement sought to be enforced against him in the suit is void by reason of his not having been competent to contract under section 11 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (9 of 1872), the court may, if the defendant has received any benefit under the agreement from the other party, require him to restore, so far as may be, such benefit to that party, to the extent to which he or his estate has benefited thereby.
- Indian Partnership Act, 1932, Section 30(1). A person who is a minor according to the law to which he is subject may not be a partner in a firm, but, with the consent of all the partners for the time being, he may be admitted to the benefits of partnership.
- In simple words
- Sections 2(g), 10, 11: a minor is not "competent to contract ", so his agreement is not a contract. It is an agreement not enforceable by law, that is, void. Section 68: a person who supplies necessaries to a minor is repaid out of the minor's property , not by the minor personally. Section 184: a minor can act as an agent for others, but he cannot be made answerable to his principal. Specific Relief Act, Section 33(2)(b): if a minor wins a suit because his agreement is void, the court may make him give back the benefit he received, but only to the extent he or his estate is still better off. Partnership Act, Section 30: a minor cannot be a partner, but can be admitted to the benefits of a partnership.

## Meaning and Definition
A minor's agreement is an agreement in which one of the parties had not completed the age of majority (generally 18) on the date it was made.

The Act does not say "void" in so many words. The Privy Council reached that result in Mohori Bibee by reading Sections 2, 10 and 11 together, and by looking at Sections 68, 183, 184, 64 and 65 (see Diagram 1 ).

Section 10: only agreements by competent parties are contracts. Section 2(g): an agreement not enforceable by law is void. Sections 68, 183 and 184 show the Act treats minors as unable to bind themselves. Sections 64 and 65 assume a contract between competent parties, so they do not apply. Result: a minor's agreement is void ab initio; no estoppel where the truth is known; no order to repay.

Void ab initio means void from the start. The agreement never was a contract. Nobody needs to cancel it, and nobody can make it good later.

Why the law takes this strict view.

To protect minors. A young person may not understand a bargain and may be easily exploited. Making every agreement void is the strongest protection. Because it follows from the Act. Section 10 requires "parties competent to contract". If one party is not competent, there is no contract to call voidable. To keep the rule simple. Lenders and sellers are told plainly: deal with a minor at your own risk.

What is "the effect"? The topic asks what follows from this voidness. The answer has many parts: no suit on the agreement, no ratification, no estoppel , limited restitution , necessaries, benefits to the minor, guardian's contracts, sureties, and the minor as partner, agent and shareholder. Each is explained below.

## Key Points to Remember
- A minor's agreement is void ab initio ( Mohori Bibee , 1903). It cannot be ratified on attaining majority; a fresh agreement with fresh consideration is needed ( Suraj Narain Dube ). A false statement of age does not estop the minor ( Mohori Bibee ; Ajudhia Prasad ). Sections 64 and 65 do not apply; restitution only under Specific Relief Act Section 33, and only to the extent of benefit. Necessaries: reimbursed from the minor's property, never a personal liability (Section 68). A minor can take a benefit, for example as a mortgagee who has paid in full ( Raghava Chariar ). A minor can be an agent but is not responsible to his principal (Section 184). A minor can only be admitted to the benefits of a partnership (Partnership Act Section 30). A guardian's contract binds the minor if within the guardian's competence and for the minor's benefit ( Srikakulam ). On a surety for a minor's debt, give both views ( Edavan ; Kashiba ).
