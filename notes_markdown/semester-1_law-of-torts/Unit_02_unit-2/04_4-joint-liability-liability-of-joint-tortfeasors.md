# 4. Joint Liability – Liability of Joint Tortfeasors
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/joint-liability/

## In One Minute
Sometimes more than one person causes the same harm. Each wrongdoer is then liable for the whole damage. The victim may proceed against all of them or against any one of them. This is joint and several liability. The one who pays may claim a share from the others. Exams ask for this as a short note, or as a problem on two drivers or on an owner and an insurer.

## Legal Provision
- Order I, rules 3, 3A, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10(2) and 13, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: all persons may be joined as defendants where a right to relief arising out of the same act or transaction is alleged against them, "jointly, severally or in the alternative", and a common question of law or fact would arise (rule 3). The court may order separate trials if joining defendants may embarrass or delay the trial (rule 3A). Judgment may be given against such one or more defendants as are found liable, "according to their respective liabilities" (rule 4). A defendant need not be interested in all the relief claimed (rule 5). A plaintiff may join, at his option, all or any persons severally, or jointly and severally, liable on one contract (rule 6). A plaintiff in doubt about who is liable may join two or more defendants, so that the question of who is liable, and to what extent, is decided between all parties (rule 7). No suit is defeated by misjoinder or non-joinder, but this does not apply to non-joinder of a necessary party (rule 9). The court may strike out an improperly joined party, or add a person who ought to have been joined, at any stage (rule 10(2)). Objections to non-joinder or misjoinder must be taken at the earliest opportunity, or they are treated as waived (rule 13). Sections 42 to 45, Indian Contract Act, 1872: these deal with joint promises. Section 42 says joint promisors must all fulfil the promise unless a contrary intention appears. Under section 43 the promisee may compel any one or more joint promisors to perform the whole promise. Each promisor may compel every other to contribute equally, unless a contrary intention appears. If one makes default in contribution, the rest bear the loss in equal shares. Section 44 says a release of one joint promisor does not discharge the others, and does not free the released promisor from responsibility to the others. Section 45 deals with joint rights. These sections speak of promises, not torts. This note uses them only as an analogy , and no Act in this syllabus sets a share for tortfeasors. Sections 25 and 26, Indian Partnership Act, 1932: every partner is liable, jointly with all the other partners and also severally, for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner (section 25). The firm is liable to the same extent as the partner for loss caused to a third party by the wrongful act of a partner in the ordinary course of the firm's business, or with the authority of his partners (section 26). Section 168(1), Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: the Claims Tribunal must give notice of the claim to the insurer and hear the parties, including the insurer. In its award it "shall specify the amount which shall be paid by the insurer or owner or driver of the vehicle involved in the accident or by all or any of them, as the case may be".

## Meaning and Definition
Joint tortfeasors are two or more persons who share in one wrong. In this note's explanation, the wrong may be done together by agreement, or one person may answer for another, as a firm answers for its partner or an employer for an employee.

In a second kind of case, two or more persons are negligent separately, and their negligence together injures one victim. The Supreme Court calls this composite negligence and says: "Where a person is injured as a result of negligence on the part of two or more wrong doers, it is said that the person was injured on account of the composite negligence of those wrong-doers" (T.O. Anthony v. Karvarnan, 2008, para 6).

For composite negligence the Court says each wrongdoer "is jointly and severally liable to the injured for payment of the entire damages" (T.O. Anthony, para 6). A partner is likewise liable "jointly with all the other partners and also severally" (Partnership Act, section 25). In this note's words, "jointly" means all can be sued together, and "severally" means each can be sued alone.

## Key Points to Remember
- Each of two or more wrongdoers who injure a third party is jointly and severally liable for the entire damages (T.O. Anthony, para 6). The injured person may proceed against all or any of them, and need not prove each one's share. Composite negligence differs from contributory negligence. If the claimant is partly at fault, composite negligence does not apply. Owner and insurer may be made jointly and severally liable (Minu B. Mehta). The Tribunal specifies who pays what (section 168(1)). Judgment may be given against defendants "according to their respective liabilities" (Order I, rule 4). The 1956 Law Commission report, quoted in Kaushal Kishor, records that in India there is no legal impediment to one tortfeasor recovering compensation from another. The Merryweather rule does not apply. In contract, joint promisors contribute equally (section 43). A release of one does not discharge the others (section 44).
