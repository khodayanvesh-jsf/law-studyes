# 7. Occupiers liability
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/occupiers-liability/

## In One Minute
In the cases below, the person who owns and controls a place or structure answered for an injury connected with its state. The claims were decided as questions of negligence. Two decisions carry this topic. In Glasgow Corporation v. Taylor (1921) a municipality had to face trial over a poisonous shrub that tempted a child in a public park. In Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti (1966) the Supreme Court held a Corporation liable when its old clock tower in Chandni Chowk collapsed and killed three persons.

## Legal Provision
- Section 1A, Fatal Accidents Act, 1855 (the 1966 judgment calls it section 1; it was renumbered 1A in 1951). If a wrongful act, neglect or default causes death, and the injured person could have sued had he lived, the party who would have been liable is liable to a suit for damages . The suit is for the benefit of the wife, husband, parent and child. It is brought by and in the name of the executor , administrator or representative of the dead person. The Court gives damages "proportioned to the loss resulting from such death". Section 2, Fatal Accidents Act: only one suit may be brought for the same subject-matter of complaint. The executor or administrator may add a claim for the pecuniary loss to the estate of the dead person. Section 4, Fatal Accidents Act: the word "person" applies to bodies politic and corporate , so a Corporation can be sued. "Parent" includes father, mother and grandparents. "Child" includes son, daughter, grandchildren and step-children.

## Meaning and Definition
Occupier is the word for the person in occupation of premises. The rulings in this note look at "ownership and control". In Subhagwanti the clock tower was "exclusively under the ownership and control" of the Corporation. In Wringe v. Cohen, which the Supreme Court followed, the owner had let his house to a tenant . The Court of Appeal said that if premises on a highway become dangerous through want of repair, "the occupier or the owner if he has undertaken the duty of repair, is answerable". So an owner who has taken on the repairs is answerable too.

Occupier's liability is the name for liability for injury connected with the state of premises. The cases here were decided as questions of negligence and of the duty of care .

The cases in this note concern a public park open to the public, and a structure beside a public highway. In Glasgow it was admitted that the child had a right to be in the park.

## Key Points to Remember
- In these cases liability followed ownership and control of the place or structure. An owner who undertakes the repairs is answerable too (Wringe v. Cohen). Glasgow Corporation v. Taylor: hidden, alluring danger known to the owner means restrict access or warn. Obvious dangers carry no special responsibility. Parents must act reasonably too. Lord Shaw: no defence of contributory negligence for the unwarned danger. Lord Sumner left carelessness of child or parent for trial. Subhagwanti: the owner of a structure beside a highway is liable for patent and latent defects. Res ipsa loquitur applies to a thing in the defendant's exclusive control that falls without a natural cause. A death claim lies under section 1A of the Fatal Accidents Act, 1855, by the representative, for the wife, husband, parent and child.
