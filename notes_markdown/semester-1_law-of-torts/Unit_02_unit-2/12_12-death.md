# 12. Death

## In One Minute
Death raises two questions in tort. First, does a claim survive when the claimant or the wrongdoer dies? The old rule said a personal action died with the person. Section 306 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 now saves most claims. Purely personal claims still end with the person. Second, what can the family claim when a wrong causes a death? The Fatal Accidents Act, 1855 gives the wife, husband, parent and child a right to sue. The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 gives a similar claim for road deaths. The exam statement death creates or extinguishes liability refers to these two points.

## Legal Provision
- Section 306, Indian Succession Act, 1925 (demands and rights of action of or against a deceased survive to and against the executor or administrator): all demands and all rights to prosecute or defend an action or special proceeding that exist at the time of a person's death survive. They survive to and against his executors or administrators . There are two exceptions. First, causes of action for defamation, assault (as defined in the Indian Penal Code, 1860) or other personal injuries not causing the death of the party. Second, cases where, after the death of the party, the relief sought could not be enjoyed or granting it would be nugatory . Illustration (i): a passenger is severely hurt in a railway collision, but not so as to cause death, and dies without suing. The cause of action does not survive. Illustration (ii): A sues for divorce and dies. The cause of action does not survive to his representative. (Text as set out in Kumud Lall v. Suresh Chandra Roy (2026), para 48.) Section 1A, Fatal Accidents Act, 1855 (called section 1 in Subhagwanti): a suit may be brought for the benefit of the wife, husband, parent and child of the deceased. This applies where death is caused by a wrongful act, neglect or default that would have given the deceased a right to sue. The damages are proportioned to the loss resulting to them. As quoted in Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti (1966), the suit lies even if the death occurred in circumstances amounting to a crime. It is brought in the name of the executor, administrator or representative, and the sum recovered, after costs, is divided among the beneficiaries as the court directs. Section 2, Fatal Accidents Act: only one suit may be brought for the same subject-matter of complaint. The executor, administrator or representative may add a claim for the pecuniary loss to the estate of the deceased. Any sum recovered for it forms part of the assets of the estate. Section 3, Fatal Accidents Act: the plaint must give full particulars of the persons for whom the suit is brought and of the nature of the claim for which damages are sought. Section 4, Fatal Accidents Act: "person" includes bodies politic and corporate . "Parent" includes father, mother and grandparents. "Child" includes son, daughter, grandchildren and step-children. Article 82, Limitation Act, 1963: a suit by the executor, administrator or representative under the Fatal Accidents Act must be filed within two years from the date of death. Sections 165 and 166, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: a Claims Tribunal decides claims for death in motor accidents. An application can be made by all or any of the legal representatives of the deceased. Section 164, Motor Vehicles Act: Rs 5 lakh is payable on death without proof of fault. Order XXII, rule 1, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: a suit does not abate by the death of a party if the right to sue survives. Order XXII, rules 3, 4, 4A, 5, 6 and 9, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: the court makes the legal representative a party when a plaintiff or defendant dies. Failure to apply in time leads to abatement . Rule 9 bars a fresh suit on the same cause of action after abatement. The sub-section on procedure below gives the detail. Section 2(11), Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: a legal representative is "a person who in law represents the estate of a deceased person". The term also includes a person who intermeddles with the estate, and the person on whom the estate devolves where a party sues or is sued in a representative character. Articles 120 and 121, Limitation Act, 1963: 90 days from the date of death to have the legal representative made a party. 60 days from the date of abatement to apply to set the abatement aside. Legal Representatives' Suits Act, 1855: in Kumud Lall (2026) the Supreme Court says suits under this Act can be filed only for causes that concern the pecuniary interest of the deceased's estate (para 57). Section 155, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (effect of death on certain causes of action): section 306 of the Succession Act does not stand in the way. Take a person who held a certificate of insurance and who dies after the event that gave rise to a claim under Chapter XI. His death does not bar the survival of any cause of action arising out of that event. The claim lies against his estate or against the insurer . Section 164(2), Motor Vehicles Act: in a claim under section 164(1), the claimant need not plead or prove that the death was due to any wrongful act, neglect or default of the owner of the vehicle or any other person. Section 166(1)(c) and first proviso, Motor Vehicles Act: where death has resulted from the accident, the application may be made "by all or any of the legal representatives of the deceased". If all of them do not join, the application is made for the benefit of all, and those who did not join are impleaded as respondents. Section 166(3) and (5), Motor Vehicles Act: an application for compensation must be made within six months of the accident. The Tribunal may entertain a later one, within a further twelve months, if sufficient cause prevented the applicant. Section 166(5) says that, notwithstanding any other law, the right of a person to claim compensation for injury in an accident survives, on the death of the person injured, to his legal representatives. It survives whether or not the cause of death has any link with the injury. Section 168(1), Motor Vehicles Act: after notice to the insurer and a hearing, the Claims Tribunal may make an award of the compensation that appears to it to be just. It specifies the persons to whom compensation is paid and the amount payable by the insurer, owner or driver.

## Meaning and Definition
The maxim actio personalis moritur cum persona means that a personal action dies with the person. At common law , a claim in tort ended if either the wrongdoer or the injured person died. It also meant that killing a person gave his family no claim at all.

The rule caused injustice. A wrongdoer who killed a person outright was in a better legal position than one who only injured him. Legislation has reversed this in two steps. It lets most claims survive, and it gives the family a new claim for a death caused by a wrong.

## Key Points to Remember
- Old rule: actio personalis moritur cum persona. Section 306, Indian Succession Act, 1925 saves most claims, for and against the estate. Exceptions: defamation, assault and personal injury not causing death. After Kumud Lall (2026), personal claims abate but claims for or against the estate continue. Order XXII CPC: apply within 90 days of death to bring in the legal representative (Article 120). The suit abates if no application is made. Fatal Accidents Act, 1855, section 1A: the representative sues for the benefit of the wife, husband, parent and child. Only one suit lies (section 2). Limit: two years (Article 82). Motor Vehicles Act: legal representatives claim before the Claims Tribunal. A brother can be one (Ramanbhai, 1987). A defamation claim does not survive the death of the person defamed (section 306).
