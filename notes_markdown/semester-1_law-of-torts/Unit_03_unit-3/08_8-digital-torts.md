# 8. Digital Torts
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 3:** Unit 3
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/digital-torts/

## In One Minute
A digital tort is an ordinary tort committed through computers, the internet, social media or other technology. India has no single statute for it. Courts apply the old torts to online facts, and the Information Technology Act, 2000 adds civil remedies and rules for platforms. In exams, this topic is asked as "explain digital torts with examples", and as a problem on an online post, a data leak or a platform's liability. The usual trap is to say that a platform is always liable for what users post.

## Legal Provision
- Information Technology Act, 2000, section 43: compensation to a person whose computer, system or data is accessed without permission, copied, damaged or disrupted. Section 43 covers these acts without permission of the owner or person in charge: accessing a computer, system or network; downloading, copying or extracting data; introducing a computer contaminant or virus; damaging a computer, data or programs; disrupting a system; denying access to an authorised person; helping another gain access; charging one's services to another's account; and destroying, deleting or altering information or diminishing its value. It also covers stealing, concealing, destroying or altering computer source code with intent to cause damage. Section 43A: a body corporate that handles sensitive personal data and is negligent in keeping reasonable security practices, causing wrongful loss or gain to any person, must pay compensation. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 omits this section from the date it is brought into force. Section 46: an Adjudicating Officer decides claims for compensation. Under section 46(1A) the Officer decides claims up to five crore rupees. Claims above that amount go to the competent court. Section 66: doing any act referred to in section 43 dishonestly or fraudulently is punishable with up to three years, or fine up to five lakh rupees, or both. Section 66E: capturing, publishing or transmitting the image of a private area of a person without consent, violating privacy: up to three years, or fine up to two lakh rupees, or both. Section 67: publishing or transmitting obscene material in electronic form: up to three years and fine up to five lakh rupees on first conviction. Second or later conviction: up to five years and fine up to ten lakh rupees. Section 79: a conditional safe harbour for an intermediary (a platform that hosts or carries content posted by others). Section 79(2) and (3): the protection applies if the intermediary only gives access, does not start the transmission, choose the receiver or modify the content, and observes due diligence. It is lost if the intermediary conspired, abetted or aided the unlawful act. It is also lost if, after actual knowledge or notice from the appropriate Government or its agency, it fails to remove or disable access to the material quickly. Limitation Act, 1963, Article 75: a suit for compensation for libel must be filed within one year from when the libel is published. Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023: sets duties on those who process personal data and gives rights to the person whose data it is. Section 6 requires consent that is free, specific and informed. Sections 11 to 13 give rights of access, correction, erasure and grievance redressal. The Data Protection Board imposes penalties under section 33. Section 39 bars civil courts from hearing matters the Board is empowered to decide. Section 44(2) omits section 43A of the Information Technology Act. Section 66A: struck down as unconstitutional in Shreya Singhal. Article 21, Constitution of India: privacy is a fundamental right. Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023: online defamation (section 356), stalking (section 78) and criminal intimidation (section 351) are also offences.

## Meaning and Definition
A digital tort is a civil wrong committed using computers, the internet, social media or other digital technology. It is not a separate category in Indian law. The ordinary elements of a tort still apply: a legal right or duty, a wrongful act or omission, and legal damage.

## Key Points to Remember
- Digital torts are ordinary torts committed online. Online defamation is libel. Forwarding repeats publication. Privacy is a fundamental right (Puttaswamy). Section 43 gives compensation. Section 46 provides an Adjudicating Officer. Section 79 safe harbour. Actual knowledge means a court order or government notification. Five characteristics: digital, wide reach, hidden wrongdoer, persistence, cross-border. Five elements: wrongful act, duty, breach, causation, damages.
