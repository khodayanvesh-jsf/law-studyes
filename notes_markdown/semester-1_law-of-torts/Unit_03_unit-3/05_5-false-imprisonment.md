# 5. False Imprisonment
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 3:** Unit 3
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/false-imprisonment/

## In One Minute
False imprisonment, in this note, means stopping a person from leaving a place without lawful justification. The leading case is Bird v. Jones (1845). It says the restraint must be total. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 deals with the criminal side in sections 126 and 127. In India the Constitution does most of the work. Articles 21 and 22 set the rules on arrest and detention. The Supreme Court and the High Courts award money compensation under Articles 32 and 226. The sources used here are the Constitution, the two Sanhitas, the Limitation Act and seven decided cases.

## Legal Provision
- The Constitution of India
- Article 21: no person is deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Article 22(1): a person who is arrested must not be detained without being told the grounds of arrest as soon as may be. The person cannot be denied the right to consult, and be defended by, a lawyer of choice. Article 22(2): every person arrested and detained must be produced before the nearest Magistrate within 24 hours, excluding the journey time. No one may be kept in custody beyond that without the Magistrate's authority. Article 22(3): clauses (1) and (2) do not apply to an enemy alien or to a person arrested or detained under a preventive detention law. Articles 32 and 226: the Supreme Court and the High Courts can issue writs to enforce the fundamental rights. The cases below show that this includes money compensation.
- Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023: wrongful restraint and confinement
- Section 126(1), wrongful restraint: a person who voluntarily obstructs another so as to prevent that other from proceeding in any direction in which the other has a right to proceed wrongfully restrains that other. Exception to section 126: it is not an offence to obstruct a private way over land or water, if the person in good faith believes he has a lawful right to obstruct it. The illustration is A, who blocks a path along which Z has a right to pass, not believing in good faith that he may stop it. A wrongfully restrains Z. Section 126(2): simple imprisonment up to one month, or fine up to five thousand rupees, or both. Section 127(1), wrongful confinement: wrongfully restraining a person so as to prevent that person from proceeding beyond certain circumscribing limits. Illustration (a): A makes Z go into a walled space and locks Z in. Z cannot go beyond the wall. A wrongfully confines Z. Illustration (b): A places men with firearms at the outlets of a building and tells Z they will fire if Z tries to leave. A wrongfully confines Z. Section 127(2): imprisonment up to one year, or fine up to five thousand rupees, or both. Section 127(3): confinement for three days or more, imprisonment up to three years, or fine up to ten thousand rupees, or both. Section 127(4): confinement for ten days or more, imprisonment up to five years, and fine of not less than ten thousand rupees. Section 127(5): keeping a person in confinement, knowing that a writ for the person's liberation has been duly issued, carries up to two years in addition to other punishment, and fine. Section 127(6): confining a person so that the confinement, or its place, may not be known to anyone interested in the person or to a public servant carries up to three years in addition to other punishment, and fine. Section 127(7) and (8): confining a person to extort property, a confession or information, or to force an illegal act or a claim, carries up to three years and fine.
- Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023: how a lawful arrest is made
- Section 35(1)(a): a police officer may arrest without a warrant a person who commits a cognizable offence in the officer's presence. Section 35(1)(b): for a cognizable offence punishable with up to seven years, the officer needs a reasonable complaint, credible information or reasonable suspicion. The officer must also have reason to believe the person committed it, and be satisfied that arrest is necessary for a stated purpose. The reasons must be recorded in writing. Purposes in section 35(1)(b)(ii): to prevent a further offence; for proper investigation; to prevent disappearance of evidence or tampering with it; to prevent inducement , threat or promise to witnesses; or because the person's presence in Court cannot otherwise be ensured. Section 35(1)(c): credible information of a cognizable offence punishable with more than seven years, or with death, and reason to believe the person committed it. Section 35(1)(d) to (j): other cases, such as a proclaimed offender , a person found with suspected stolen property, a person who obstructs a police officer, an escapee, and a person wanted on a requisition from another police officer. Section 35(2): for a non-cognizable offence, no arrest except under a warrant or order of a Magistrate, subject to section 39. Section 35(3) to (6): where arrest is not required, the officer must issue a notice to appear. If the person complies and continues to comply, he is not arrested unless, for reasons recorded, the officer thinks he ought to be. If he fails to comply, he may be arrested. Section 35(7): no arrest without prior permission of an officer not below Deputy Superintendent of Police, for an offence punishable with less than three years, if the person is infirm or above sixty years of age. Section 36: the arresting officer must bear a clear name identification and prepare a memorandum of arrest . It must be attested by at least one witness from the family or a respectable member of the locality, and countersigned by the arrested person. Unless a family member attested the memorandum, the officer must tell the person of the right to have a relative or friend informed. Section 38: an arrested person may meet an advocate of choice during interrogation , though not throughout the interrogation. Section 40(1): a private person may arrest, or cause to be arrested, a person who commits a non-bailable and cognizable offence in the private person's presence, or a proclaimed offender. The arrested person must be handed over to a police officer without unnecessary delay, and within six hours. Section 40(3): if a person arrested by a private person is believed to have committed a non-cognizable offence and refuses to give a name and residence, or gives a false one, the police deal with the person under section 39. If there is no sufficient reason to believe he committed any offence, he is released at once. Section 43(1): in making an arrest, the officer or other person must actually touch or confine the body, unless the person submits to custody by word or action. A woman's submission on an oral intimation of arrest is presumed, unless the circumstances indicate otherwise. Section 47(1) and (2): a person arrested without a warrant must be told the full particulars of the offence or other grounds at once. A person not accused of a non-bailable offence must be told of the right to bail . Section 48: the person making the arrest must at once inform a relative, friend or nominated person, and the designated police officer in the district. An entry must be made in a police station book, and the Magistrate must check that this was done. Sections 57 and 58: a person arrested without a warrant must be taken before a Magistrate without unnecessary delay. No one may be detained longer than is reasonable. Without a special Magistrate's order under section 187, the limit is 24 hours, excluding the journey time. Section 187: if the investigation cannot finish in 24 hours, the accused is sent to the nearest Magistrate. The Magistrate may authorise detention in such custody as he thinks fit, for up to fifteen days in all. He may go beyond fifteen days if adequate grounds exist. The total cannot exceed ninety days (offences punishable with death, life imprisonment or ten years or more) or sixty days (other offences). After that the accused is released on bail if ready to furnish it.
- General exceptions in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
- Section 14: nothing is an offence if done by a person who is bound by law to do it, or who by a mistake of fact (not of law), in good faith, believes himself bound by law to do it. Illustration (b): a Court officer ordered to arrest Y arrests Z after due inquiry, believing Z to be Y. He commits no offence. Section 15: nothing is an offence if done by a Judge acting judicially, in the exercise of a power given by law, or which he in good faith believes is given. Section 16: an act done under a Court's judgment or order, while it remains in force, is not an offence, even if the Court had no jurisdiction , if the person in good faith believes the Court had jurisdiction. Section 17: nothing is an offence if done by a person who is justified by law, or who by a mistake of fact (not of law), in good faith, believes himself justified by law. The illustration is A, who seizes Z, believing in good faith that Z is committing murder, to bring Z before the authorities. A commits no offence, though Z may have been acting in self-defence.
- Limitation
- Limitation Act, 1963, Article 73: a suit for compensation for false imprisonment must be filed within one year. The period runs from when the imprisonment ends.

## Meaning and Definition
False imprisonment is the restraint of a person's liberty, without lawful cause, within limits that the person cannot pass. In Bird v. Jones, Patteson J. agreed to this definition from Selwyn's Nisi Prius: "a restraint on the liberty of the person without lawful cause". The definition adds that it may be by confinement in a prison, stocks or house, or by forcibly detaining the party in the streets against his will.

The definition names a prison, stocks, a house or the streets as places of restraint. Coleridge J. explained that a prison "may have its boundary large or narrow, visible and tangible, or, though real, still in the conception only". But a boundary there must be, and the person must be prevented from passing it.

Restraint of a person's body is different from the mere loss of freedom to go where one pleases. Coleridge J. said that imprisonment "includes the notion of restraint within some limits defined by a will or power exterior to our own".

## Key Points to Remember
- False imprisonment is total restraint without lawful cause. Partial obstruction is not enough (Bird v. Jones). Touching, walls and a long time are not needed. BNS section 126 is wrongful restraint. Section 127 is wrongful confinement. For offences up to seven years, police arrest needs a necessity ground and recorded reasons (BNSS section 35(1)(b)). Production before a Magistrate within 24 hours is a constitutional rule (Article 22(2)). Compensation under Articles 32 and 226 is a public law remedy. Sovereign immunity does not apply (Nilabati Behera). Time limit: one year from the end of imprisonment (Article 73).
