# 7. Nervous Shock
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 3:** Unit 3
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/nervous-shock/

## In One Minute
Nervous shock means a psychiatric injury caused by what a person sees or hears, without a physical blow. Grief and sorrow alone do not give a claim. The cases here show two routes: an intentional act (Wilkinson v. Downton) and negligence (Bourhill v. Young and Alcock). For negligence, a claimant who suffers shock because of danger to someone else must pass the control tests in Alcock. The sources used here are the judgments in those cases, as read in the project's files.

## Legal Provision
- No statute is used in this note. The rules come from decided cases. Lord Wilberforce's summary in McLoughlin v. O'Brian (1983) , as set out in the Court of Appeal in Alcock, has five points. They are the starting point for the modern rules. Point 1: damages cannot, at common law, be awarded for grief and sorrow. A claim for nervous shock caused by negligence can be made without direct impact, or fear of immediate personal injury to oneself. Point 2: a plaintiff may recover for nervous shock brought on by injury caused not to himself but to a near relative, or by fear of such injury. Point 3: no English case had allowed recovery where the injury to the near relative occurred out of sight and earshot of the plaintiff. Hambrook v. Stokes Brothers drew a line between what the mother saw with her own eyes and what bystanders told her. Point 4: an extension has been made where the plaintiff does not see or hear the incident but comes upon its immediate aftermath. Point 5: a remedy has been given to a man who came upon a serious accident involving numerous people immediately afterwards and acted as a rescuer (Chadwick v. British Railways Board).

## Meaning and Definition
Nervous shock is the name the cases use for psychiatric illness caused by shock. The claimants in Alcock sought damages for psychiatric illness resulting from what they saw and heard. It is a name for the damage, not a separate act.

Lord Keith said that television scenes could not reasonably be regarded as giving rise to shock "in the sense of a sudden assault on the nervous system". They could give rise to anxiety for relatives' safety. That is different from seeing the fate of a relative, or his condition shortly after the event.

## Key Points to Remember
- Nervous shock is psychiatric illness from shock. Grief and sorrow alone are not enough. No physical impact and no fear for oneself is needed. Intentional: Wilkinson v. Downton. Negligent: duty, foreseeability, proximity. Alcock tests: close tie of love and affection, closeness in time and space, and sight or hearing of the event or its immediate aftermath. Live television was held not enough in Alcock.
