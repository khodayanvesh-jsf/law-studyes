# 9. Wrongful intent
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/wrongful-intent/

## In One Minute
Wrongful intent means that the defendant meant to do the act that caused the harm, or knew harm was almost certain. A few torts need it. Most do not. Intention is not motive. Intention is the aim of the act, and motive is the reason behind it. In exams, expect questions on this difference, on mistake as a defence, and on which torts need intent.

## Legal Provision
- No statute defines intention for tort. The meaning comes from decided cases. Rule of evidence, not of statute: a court may infer intention from the act. In Wilkinson v. Downton the judge held that an intention to produce the shock "must be imputed".

## Meaning and Definition
Wrongful intent is a state of mind in which the defendant either wished to bring about the harmful result or knew that it was virtually certain to follow. The act itself must also be wrongful. The result must be virtually certain, not merely possible.

The test looks at what the defendant wanted or knew, not at what a reasonable person would have foreseen. When a court imputes intention from the act, as in Wilkinson v. Downton, it is a way of showing what the defendant meant. The court asks whether the act was plainly calculated to produce the effect. A person who aims at a result intends it. A person who knows it will follow as a certain side effect also intends it for this purpose.

## Key Points to Remember
- Intention is the aim of the act or knowledge that the result is almost certain. Motive is the reason for acting and is generally irrelevant. Intention is inferred from the act and its natural results. Intent transfers from the intended victim to the person actually harmed. An honest mistake does not defeat trespass or conversion. Assault, battery, false imprisonment and deceit need intent. Negligence does not.
