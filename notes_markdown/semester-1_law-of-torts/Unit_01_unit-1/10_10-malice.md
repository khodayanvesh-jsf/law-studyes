# 10. Malice
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester 1) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/malice/

## In One Minute
Malice has two meanings in tort. Malice in law means a wrongful act done on purpose, without just cause. Malice in fact means ill will or an improper purpose. The general rule is that malice does not make a lawful act wrongful. Malice matters in a few torts, such as malicious prosecution, and in the defence of privilege. Examiners ask for the two meanings and for the torts where it matters.

## Legal Provision
- No statute defines malice for tort. The meaning comes from decided cases. The rule comes from decided cases. This note uses Bradford Corporation v. Pickles (House of Lords, 1895), Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow & Co. (House of Lords, 1892), Quinn v. Leathem (House of Lords, 1901) and Town Area Committee v. Prabhu Dayal (Allahabad High Court, 1974). Section 2(m), Limitation Act, 1963: "tort" means a civil wrong which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust.

## Meaning and Definition
Malice is a word with two technical senses. Courts must know which one is meant.

Malice in law. A wrongful act done intentionally, without just cause or excuse. No ill will is needed. In Wilkinson v. Downton, Wright J. said a wilful act of this kind "is in law malicious", although no motive of spite is imputed to the defendant. Malice in fact. Real spite or a sinister motive behind the act. Lord Brampton describes it in Quinn v. Leathem: "personal spite" or a wish to harm the accused, in a malicious prosecution.

Malice in law looks at the act. Malice in fact looks at the actor's mind.

## Key Points to Remember
- Malice in law: a wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse. Malice in fact: ill will, spite or improper purpose. A bad motive does not make a lawful act a tort. Malice is an element in malicious prosecution. Malice can defeat a defence of privilege. A good motive does not excuse an unlawful act (Lord Halsbury). Allen v. Flood does not protect a combination to injure a trader (Quinn v. Leathem).
