# 7. Declaratory Decrees
**Subject:** Law of Contract – I (Semester I) | **Unit 5:** Unit 5
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-i/declaratory-decrees/

## In One Minute
Not every dispute needs money or an order to act. Sometimes a person needs the court to say, on the record, what his status or right is, before anyone acts on a wrong assumption. That statement is a declaratory decree . Sections 34 and 35 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 provide it. The court grants it in its discretion (freedom to decide), and not if the plaintiff leaves out a further relief he could have claimed. In exams, you must state the conditions and explain the proviso. In real life, it is how an adopted child, a widow or a landowner gets a clear answer from the court before a family or neighbour acts against them.

## Legal Provision
- Section 34. Discretion of court as to declaration of status or right. Any person entitled to any legal character, or to any right as to any property, may institute a suit against any person denying, or interested to deny, his title to such character or right, and the court may in its discretion make therein a declaration that he is so entitled, and the plaintiff need not in such suit ask for any further relief: Provided that no court shall make any such declaration where the plaintiff, being able to seek further relief than a mere declaration of title, omits to do so. Explanation. —A trustee of property is a "person interested to deny" a title adverse to the title of some one who is not in existence, and for whom, if in existence, he would be a trustee.
- Section 35. Effect of declaration. A declaration made under this Chapter is binding only on the parties to the suit, persons claiming through them respectively, and, where any of the parties are trustees, on the persons for whom, if in existence at the date of the declaration, such parties would be trustees.
- In simple words
- Section 34: any person entitled to any legal character, or to any right as to any property, may sue a person denying, or interested to deny, his title to that character or right. The court may, in its discretion, declare that he is so entitled. The plaintiff need not ask for any further relief. Proviso to Section 34: no court shall make such a declaration where the plaintiff, being able to seek further relief than a mere declaration of title, omits to do so. Explanation to Section 34: a trustee of property is a "person interested to deny" a title adverse (opposed) to the title of someone who is not yet in existence, and for whom, if in existence, he would be a trustee. Section 35: a declaration binds only the parties to the suit and persons claiming through them. Where a party is a trustee, it also binds the persons for whom he would be a trustee, if they were in existence at the date of the declaration.

## Meaning and Definition
A declaratory decree is a court decree that states a person's legal character or right. It orders nobody to do anything. It removes a doubt.

A "legal character" is a legal status, such as being a person's lawfully adopted son or a lawful heir. A "right as to property" is a right such as ownership or an easement (a right over another's land, such as a right of way).

The question this part of the Act answers. When may a person ask the court only to say what his status or right is, and whom does that statement bind?

Why the law has this rule. A doubt about status or title can be as harmful as an actual wrong: relatives may refuse a share, buyers may refuse to buy, banks may refuse a loan. A declaration settles the doubt early, without waiting for a breach. But the proviso stops a plaintiff from taking only a hollow declaration when a real remedy was available.

Who is bound. The declaration binds the plaintiff and the defendant, and those who claim through either of them. Where a party is a trustee, it also binds the persons for whom he would be a trustee. It does not bind a stranger. See Diagram 1 .

## Key Points to Remember
- Section 34: a discretionary declaration of a legal character or a right as to property, against a person denying or interested to deny it. A bare declaration is enough only where no further relief is available. The proviso bars it otherwise. Section 35: a declaration binds only the parties and those claiming through them. The Explanation: a trustee is a person interested to deny a title adverse to a beneficiary not yet in existence. Anathula Sudhakar : a title dispute needs a declaration with the injunction. Annamalai : where there is no real cloud, a buyer may sue for specific performance without a separate declaration.
