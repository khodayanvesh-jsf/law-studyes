# 10. Exceptions to the privity of contract
**Subject:** Law of Contract – I (Semester I) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-i/exceptions-to-the-privity-of-contract/

## In One Minute
The general rule of privity is that only a party to a contract can sue on it ( Dunlop v. Selfridge ; M.C. Chacko v. State Bank of Travancore ). But the rule would cause injustice in some cases, so the courts recognise exceptions in which a person who is not a party can enforce the contract, or is treated as a party: Trust or charge : a beneficiary under a trust, or a person in whose favour a charge on property is created by the contract ( Khwaja Muhammad Khan v. Husaini Begam ). Marriage settlement, partition or other family arrangement , where provision is made for a family member ( M.C. Chacko recognises this exception). Acknowledgment or estoppel : where the promisor, by his conduct or acknowledgment, makes himself directly liable to the third person ( Narayani Devi v. Tagore Commercial Corporation ). Assignment : the benefit of a contract can be assigned, and the assignee can enforce it. Agency : a principal can sue and be sued on a contract made by his agent (Sections 182, 226, 230). Covenants running with land : a purchaser of land with notice of a restrictive covenant can be bound by it ( Tulk v. Moxhay ). In exams, marks come from stating the rule first, then each exception with its basis and a case. In real life, it tells a widow, a family member, a buyer of a debt or a principal when they can sue on a contract they did not sign.

## Legal Provision
- There is no single section listing the exceptions. They come from case law and from the provisions below.
- Section 2(c). The person making the proposal is called the "promisor", and the person accepting the proposal is called the "promisee";
- Section 2(d). When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise;
- Section 182. "Agent" and "principal" defined. An "agent" is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the "principal".
- Section 226. Enforcement and consequences of agent's contracts. Contracts entered into through an agent, and obligations arising from acts done by an agent, may be enforced in the same manner, and will have the same legal consequences, as if the contracts had been entered into and the acts done by the principal in person.
- Section 230 (first paragraph). In the absence of any contract to that effect, an agent cannot personally enforce contracts entered into by him on behalf of his principal, nor is he personally bound by them.
- The rule and its exceptions, in the words of the Supreme Court ( M.C. Chacko v. State Bank of Travancore , 1969): "It must therefore be taken as well settled that except in the case of a beneficiary under a trust created by a contract or in the case of a family arrangement, no right may be enforced by a person who is not a party to the contract."
- In simple words
- Sections 2(c), 2(d): the parties are the promisor and promisee; consideration may come from anyone. The privity rule comes from these definitions. Section 182: an agent acts for a principal . Section 226: a contract made through an agent is enforced as if the principal made it himself . So the principal, who did not sign, can sue and be sued. Section 230: the agent normally cannot sue or be sued on it. M.C. Chacko: the Supreme Court named two exceptions expressly: a beneficiary under a trust and a family arrangement . Other exceptions come from other cases and statutes.

## Meaning and Definition
What an "exception to privity" is

An exception is a situation in which a person who is not the promisee can still enforce a promise, or is bound by it, because the law treats him as having a direct right or duty.

Why the exceptions exist

Justice to the intended beneficiary. Where the whole purpose of the contract is to provide for a person, such as a wife or a widow, it would be unjust to leave her without a remedy. Equity. A trust or charge gives the beneficiary an equitable right, not a contractual one. In M.C. Chacko , the Supreme Court explained that a beneficiary under a trust may enforce the rights the trust gives him because "though he is not a party to the contract his rights are equitable and not contractual." Representation. In agency, the principal is the real party; the agent is only his instrument. Commercial need. Debts and other contract rights must be transferable ( assignment ).

Running example (the rule)

Kavya sells her car to Madhu for ₹4,00,000 , and Madhu promises Kavya to pay ₹1,00,000 of the price directly to Kavya's creditor Suresh . Madhu does not pay. Under the rule, Suresh cannot sue Madhu ( M.C. Chacko ; Jamna Das v. Ram Autar ). Below, we change the facts to fit each exception.

## Key Points to Remember
- The rule: only parties can sue ( Dunlop ; M.C. Chacko ). Trust or charge : beneficiary can enforce in equity ( Khwaja Muhammad Khan ). Family arrangement , marriage settlement or partition: family member provided for can enforce ( M.C. Chacko ). Acknowledgment or estoppel : promisor's conduct creates direct liability ( Narayani Devi ). Assignment : benefit, not burden, can be transferred (Transfer of Property Act, Section 130). Agency : principal sues and is sued (Section 226); agent normally not (Section 230). Covenants running with land : purchaser with notice bound ( Tulk v. Moxhay ). Chinnaya v. Ramayya is about consideration, not an exception to privity.
