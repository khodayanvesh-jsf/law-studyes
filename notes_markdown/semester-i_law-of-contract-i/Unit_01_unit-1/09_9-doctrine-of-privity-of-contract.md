# 9. Doctrine of Privity of Contract
**Subject:** Law of Contract – I (Semester I) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-i/doctrine-of-privity-of-contract/

## In One Minute
The doctrine of privity of contract says that only the parties to a contract can sue on it , and only they can be sued on it. A person who is not a party, a "third party" or "stranger", has no right to enforce the contract, even if it was made for his benefit. The Indian Contract Act does not state the rule in one section, but it follows from the definitions: the promisor is the person making the proposal and the promisee is the person accepting it (Section 2(c)), and Section 37 says "the parties to a contract" must perform their promises. The Supreme Court confirmed the rule in M.C. Chacko v. State Bank of Travancore (1969), following Dunlop v. Selfridge and Jamna Das v. Ram Autar . Keep two ideas apart: privity of contract (who is a party?) applies in India; but privity of consideration (must consideration come from the plaintiff?) does not , because Section 2(d) allows consideration to move from "the promisee or any other person ". There are recognised exceptions (trust or charge, family arrangement, acknowledgment, agency, assignment , covenants running with land). They are covered in Exceptions to the privity of contract . In exams, marks come from the meaning, the statutory basis, the two aspects of privity, Tweddle , Dunlop and M.C. Chacko , and a short note on exceptions. In real life, it tells you whether you can sue on a contract you did not sign.

## Legal Provision
- Section 2(b). When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise;
- Section 2(c). The person making the proposal is called the "promisor", and the person accepting the proposal is called the "promisee";
- Section 2(d). When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise;
- Section 2(e). Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other, is an agreement;
- Section 2(h). An agreement enforceable by law is a contract;
- Section 37 (first paragraph). Obligation of parties to contracts. The parties to a contract must either perform, or offer to perform, their respective promises, unless such performance is dispensed with or excused under the provisions of this Act, or of any other law.
- In simple words
- Section 2(c): the promisor is the one who makes the proposal; the promisee is the one who accepts it. These two are the parties . Section 2(d): the consideration may be given by the promisee or by someone else . Sections 2(e), 2(h): promises exchanged between the parties form an agreement; if enforceable, a contract . Section 37: it is the parties who must perform; the duty and the right lie between them. Read together, these sections make the contract a link between the parties . A third person is outside that link.

## Meaning and Definition
What "privity" means

"Privity" means a relationship that exists between the parties to a transaction. Privity of contract is the relationship between the parties to a contract. The doctrine of privity draws two consequences from it:

A stranger to a contract cannot sue on it , even if the contract was made for his benefit. A stranger to a contract cannot be sued on it : the parties cannot impose duties on a person who did not agree.

The two aspects of privity

Aspect Question English law Indian law Privity of contract Is the plaintiff a party to the contract? Only a party can sue ( Dunlop ) Only a party can sue ( M.C. Chacko ; Jamna Das ) Privity of consideration Did the consideration move from the plaintiff? It must ( Tweddle ; Dunlop ) Not needed: "the promisee or any other person" (Section 2(d))

So in India a promisee can sue even if a third person gave the consideration; but a third person who is not a promisee cannot sue, even if he gave consideration or was meant to benefit. The Calcutta High Court explained this in Krishna Lal Sadhu v. Pramila Bala Dasi (Rankin CJ), quoted with approval by the Supreme Court in M.C. Chacko : Section 2(d) widens consideration, but "there is nothing in s. 2 to encourage the idea that contracts can be enforced by a person who is not a party to the contract, but this notion is rightly excluded by the definition of 'promisor' and 'promisee'."

Why the law has this rule

Freedom of contract. The parties chose each other. A stranger should not be able to step in and enforce, or be burdened by, a bargain he did not make. Mutuality. In Tweddle v. Atkinson , Crompton J said it would be "a monstrous proposition to say that a person was a party to the contract for the purpose of suing upon it for his own advantage, and not a party to it for the purpose of being sued." Freedom to vary. The parties may wish to change or cancel their contract. If third parties had rights, they could not do so freely.

Running example

Kavya sells her car to Madhu for ₹4,00,000 . As part of the price, Madhu promises Kavya that he will pay ₹1,00,000 directly to Suresh , to whom Kavya owes money, and pay the balance of ₹3,00,000 to Kavya (₹1,00,000 + ₹3,00,000 = ₹4,00,000). Madhu pays Kavya ₹3,00,000 but never pays Suresh.

Parties: Kavya (promisee of Madhu's promise to pay) and Madhu (promisor). Third party: Suresh, who benefits from the promise but did not make or accept it.

## Key Points to Remember
- Only parties to a contract can sue or be sued on it. Statutory basis: promisor and promisee are the parties (Section 2(c)); parties must perform (Section 37). Tweddle v. Atkinson (1861): stranger to consideration cannot sue. Dunlop v. Selfridge (1915): "only a person who is a party to a contract can sue on it." M.C. Chacko (1969): rule applies in India, except a beneficiary under a trust or a family arrangement. Privity of consideration does not apply in India (Section 2(d)); privity of contract does. The promisee can sue even if the benefit was for a third person. Exceptions: trust/charge, family arrangement, acknowledgment, assignment, agency, covenants running with land.
