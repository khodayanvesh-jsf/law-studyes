# 3. Delivery of goods
**Subject:** Law of Contracts – II (Semester II) | **Unit 4:** Unit 4
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-ii/delivery-of-goods/

## In One Minute
"Delivery" means voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another (section 2(2)). Under section 33, delivery of goods sold may be made by doing anything which the parties agree shall be treated as delivery, or which has the effect of putting the goods in the possession of the buyer or of any person authorised to hold them on his behalf. Sections 34 to 40 give the detailed rules: part delivery, the buyer's application, place and time, delivery of the wrong quantity, instalments, delivery to a carrier or wharfinger, and risk where goods go to a distant place. Key points for the answer: the meaning of delivery, then each rule with its section.

## Meaning and Definition
What delivery is (sections 2(2) and 33)

Rule. Delivery is the voluntary transfer of possession. It may be made by doing anything the parties agree shall be treated as delivery, or anything which has the effect of putting the goods in the possession of the buyer or of a person authorised to hold them for him. The Act does not use the words "actual", "constructive" or "symbolic" delivery.

Example. A seller hands the goods over the counter to the buyer. The goods are in the buyer's possession, so delivery is made. If the parties agree that handing over the keys of the godown shall count as delivery, that too is delivery under section 33.

Part delivery (section 34)

Rule. Delivery of part, in progress of the delivery of the whole, has the same effect for passing the property as delivery of the whole. Delivery of part with an intention of severing it from the whole does not operate as delivery of the remainder.

When the seller must deliver (sections 35 and 36)

Rule. Unless the contract says otherwise, the seller need not deliver until the buyer applies for delivery (section 35). Section 36 then gives these defaults.

Whether the buyer takes possession or the seller sends the goods depends on the contract. Apart from it, the place of delivery is where the goods are at the time of the sale or agreement, or, if not then in existence, where they are manufactured or produced (section 36(1)). If the seller must send the goods but no time is fixed, he must send them within a reasonable time (section 36(2)). If a third person holds the goods, there is no delivery until he acknowledges to the buyer that he holds them on the buyer's behalf. This does not affect the issue or transfer of a document of title (section 36(3)). Demand or tender of delivery may be treated as ineffectual unless made at a reasonable hour, a question of fact (section 36(4)). Unless otherwise agreed, the seller bears the expenses of putting the goods into a deliverable state (section 36(5)).

Wrong quantity and instalments (sections 37 and 38)

Rule. If less than the contract quantity is delivered, the buyer may reject, or accept and pay at the contract rate. If more is delivered, he may accept the contract quantity and reject the rest, reject the whole, or accept the whole and pay at the contract rate. If contract goods are mixed with goods of a different description, he may accept the contract goods and reject the rest, or reject the whole (section 37). The buyer is not bound to accept delivery by instalments unless otherwise agreed. For separately paid instalments, whether a default is a repudiation of the whole or a severable breach depends on the terms of the contract and the circumstances (section 38).

Example. Kiran contracts to buy 100 kg of sugar. The seller delivers 120 kg. Kiran may take 100 kg and reject 20 kg, or reject all 120 kg, or accept all and pay at the contract rate (section 37(2)).

Delivery to a carrier, and distant places (sections 39 and 40)

Rule. Where the seller is authorised or required to send the goods, delivery to a carrier or wharfinger is prima facie delivery to the buyer. Unless authorised otherwise, the seller must make a reasonable contract with the carrier or wharfinger on the buyer's behalf. If he omits to do so and the goods are lost or damaged in transit or custody, the buyer may decline to treat it as delivery to himself or hold the seller responsible in damages. For sea transit where it is usual to insure, the seller must give the buyer notice enabling him to insure, failing which the goods are at the seller's risk during sea transit (section 39). If the seller agrees to deliver at his own risk at a distant place, the buyer takes, unless otherwise agreed, any risk of deterioration necessarily incident to transit (section 40).

Has delivery been made? How to read this: start at the top. 1. Did the parties agree something shall count as delivery, or are the goods in the buyer's possession or that of a person authorised to hold them for him (section 33)? Delivery is made. 2. Are the goods held by a third person (section 36(3))? No delivery until he acknowledges to the buyer that he holds them on the buyer's behalf. 3. Were the goods sent to a carrier or wharfinger as the contract required (section 39)? Prima facie delivery to the buyer. 4. Does the quantity or description match the contract (section 37)? If not, the buyer may accept or reject as section 37 allows.

## Key Points to Remember
- Delivery is the voluntary transfer of possession (section 2(2)). It may be made by what the parties agree, or by putting the goods in the buyer's possession (section 33). The seller need not deliver until the buyer applies (section 35). Third person holds the goods: no delivery until he acknowledges to the buyer (section 36(3)). Wrong quantity: the buyer's options are in section 37. Instalments: section 38. Delivery to a carrier is prima facie delivery to the buyer (section 39).
