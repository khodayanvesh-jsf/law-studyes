# 1. Indemnity and Guarantee-Contract of Indemnity, definition
**Subject:** Law of Contracts – II (Semester II) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-ii/contract-of-indemnity/

## In One Minute
A contract of indemnity is a contract in which one person promises to make good the loss of another. The person who promises is the promisor . The person who is protected is the promisee . Section 124 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 defines it. The loss may be caused by the promisor's own conduct or by the conduct of any other person. Key points for the answer: the definition word for word, the two sources of loss, and the Act's illustration.

## Meaning and Definition
The meaning of the definition (section 124)

Rule. A contract of indemnity is a contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss. The party who promises is the promisor. The party who is promised protection is the promisee. Section 124 uses these two words.

Take the definition in parts, as you would in the exam.

It is a contract. Section 124 begins "A contract by which...". One party makes a promise. The promise is to save the other party from loss. The loss is caused to the other party by conduct. The section names whose conduct it may be (see below).

Example. Rao owns a shop in Guntur. Sita tells Rao: "If anyone takes proceedings against you over the cheque you accepted from my brother, I will make good your loss." Sita is the promisor and Rao is the promisee. The promise is to save Rao from loss, so it is a contract of indemnity.

First source of loss: the conduct of the promisor himself

Rule. The loss may be caused to the promisee by the conduct of the promisor himself. The promisor then promises to save the promisee from the results of the promisor's own acts.

Example. Kumar sells a cart-load of grain to Latha and promises: "If my earlier sale of this grain to anyone else causes you loss, I will make it good." The loss would come from Kumar's own conduct. The promise fits section 124.

Second source of loss: the conduct of any other person

Rule. The loss may be caused to the promisee by the conduct of any other person. The section says "any other person". In the Act's illustration, C is a third person.

The Act's own illustration is of this kind. A promises B protection against the consequences of proceedings that C may take against B for a sum of 200 rupees. C is a third person. The loss would come from C's conduct in taking proceedings. The Act says this is a contract of indemnity.

Example. Dev agrees to indemnify Esha against any proceedings that Farid may take against Esha for Rs 200. Farid sues Esha. The loss comes from Farid's conduct, not from Dev's. Dev's promise is still a contract of indemnity. This is the Act's own illustration with new names.

Is section 124 the whole law of indemnity?

Rule. No. Sections 124 and 125 are not exhaustive of the law of indemnity. The Contract Act is both an amending and a consolidating Act. It does not contain the whole law of contract applied by Indian courts ( Gajanan Moreshwar , para 3).

What section 124 covers. One kind of indemnity only: a promise to save the other from loss caused by the conduct of the promisor or of any other person. What it does not cover. Indemnity for loss caused by events or accidents that do not or may not depend on the conduct of the promisor or any other person. It also does not cover a liability the indemnified person incurred by doing something at the request of the indemnifier (para 3). What section 125 covers. Only the rights of the indemnity-holder when he is sued. The indemnity-holder has other rights besides those in section 125 (para 4). Application. In Gajanan Moreshwar the plaintiff became liable by doing something at the defendant's request. So the Court held that section 124 did not apply at all (para 4). Result. The Indian courts apply the same equitable principles that the courts in England apply (para 7).

When can the indemnity-holder sue?

Old common-law rule. Under English common law, the indemnity-holder could not sue until he had actually suffered loss. This was hard on him. If he was sued, he had to wait for the judgment and pay it first. If he could not pay, he could not use his indemnity at all ( Gajanan Moreshwar , para 7).

The rule of equity. Equity softened this. Once the holder's liability has become absolute, he can make the indemnifier pay off the claim. He can also make the indemnifier pay enough money into Court to meet the claim when it is made (para 7).

The test used in India. The Court asks two questions. Has the indemnified person incurred a liability? Is that liability absolute? If yes to both, he can call on the indemnifier to save him from the liability and pay it off. He need not wait to suffer actual loss ( Gajanan Moreshwar , paras 5 and 7; approved in Khetarpal Amarnath , quoted in VPS Healthcare , para 19).

Absolute and contingent liability. A liability is absolute when the promisee has no answer to a claim on it, for example where he has personally promised to pay ( Gajanan Moreshwar , para 8). The defendant in Gajanan Moreshwar argued that the liability was only contingent, because a sale of the mortgaged property might leave no deficit. The Court rejected this (para 8).

Absolute obligation and indemnity. A promise to take on an absolute obligation can be enforced without any actual loss. In a true indemnity, the risk of loss stays contingent. A contract of indemnity is one in which a party undertakes to save another party harmless from loss or damage arising from a particular transaction or claim ( VPS Healthcare , para 19). So the wording of the promise decides whether it is an indemnity or an absolute obligation (para 19).

Contrary decisions. Two earlier decisions had said that a suit on an indemnity before actual loss is premature: Shankar Nimbaji v. Laxman Supdu (1939) 42 Bom LR 175 and Chand Bibi v. Santoshkumar Pal (1933) ILR 60 Cal 761. Chagla J. confined the first to its own facts, where no loss could be known until the security was sold. He would not read it to mean that actual loss is always needed (para 5). He did not follow the second. He called it an unconsidered judgment, with no authorities cited, that may turn on the wording of the covenant. He preferred Osman Jamal & Sons v. Gopal Purshattam (1928) ILR 56 Cal 262, a considered decision that allowed the suit before payment (para 6). It was also conceded in Gajanan Moreshwar that such a suit would lie in England (para 7).

What the Court may order. The Court may direct the indemnifier to get the promisee released from the liability within a fixed time. If he fails, he may be directed to pay into Court the amount needed to meet it ( Gajanan Moreshwar , para 10).

How to test a promise against section 124 How to read this: start at the top and answer each box. If you can answer yes to all three, the promise is a contract of indemnity under section 124. 1. Is there a contract? 2. Does one party promise to save the other from loss? That party is the promisor. The other is the promisee. 3. Is the loss caused by the promisor's own conduct, or by the conduct of any other person? If yes, it is a contract of indemnity (section 124).

## Key Points to Remember
- Section 124 defines a contract of indemnity. One party promises to save the other from loss. The loss may be caused by the conduct of the promisor himself or of any other person. The party who promises is the promisor. The party protected is the promisee. The Act's illustration: A indemnifies B against proceedings C may take against B for 200 rupees. The rights of the promisee when he is sued are in section 125.
