# 8. Rights and liabilities of Surety
**Subject:** Law of Contracts – II (Semester II) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-ii/rights-and-liabilities-of-surety/

## In One Minute
A surety stands behind a borrower. The lender is the creditor . The borrower is the principal debtor . The surety's duty to pay is as wide as the borrower's. The lender may ask the surety to pay at once. After paying, the surety gets rights against the borrower, the lender's securities and other sureties. Key points for the answer: the section 128 rule, the case Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad and these three rights.

## Meaning and Definition
Liability: as wide as the debtor's (section 128)

Rule. The surety is liable for as much as the principal debtor owes, including interest and charges that fall due. The only limit is what the contract provides. This is what coextensive means in section 128.

The bond may cap the amount, as in Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad (Rs. 12,000 and interest). Section 145 separately limits what the surety can recover from the borrower: no more than the borrower really owed.

Example. Meena guarantees a bank loan of Rs 2 lakh taken by her brother Ravi in Vijayawada. Ravi defaults . Meena is liable for the whole Rs 2 lakh and the interest due. If her bond had capped her at Rs 1 lakh with interest, that cap would govern. The Act's own illustration holds a guarantor of a dishonoured bill of exchange liable for the amount, the interest and the charges.

Liability: immediate, with no need to sue the debtor first

Rule. The creditor does not have to sue the principal debtor first. In Bank of Bihar the bond made demand the only condition. Once the creditor demanded payment and it was not met, the surety was liable at once. A guarantee may set other conditions, so always read its terms. The Supreme Court said the liability was immediate. It was not deferred until the creditor had exhausted his remedies against the principal debtor.

The surety cannot tell the creditor to chase the debtor first. In the absence of some special equity, the surety also cannot restrain execution against him on the ground that the debtor is solvent.

Example. This is what happened in Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad (see the case below). The Supreme Court set aside an order that made the bank exhaust its remedies against the borrower first.

Liability: delay by the creditor does not free the surety (section 137)

Rule. Mere forbearance by the creditor to sue the principal debtor does not discharge the surety. The guarantee may say otherwise, and then it governs.

Example. Kiran owes Mohan a debt guaranteed by Suresh. The debt becomes payable. Mohan does not sue Kiran for a year. Suresh is not discharged. This is the Act's own illustration to section 137.

Liability: two persons liable together, one acting as surety only between themselves (section 132)

Rule. Two persons may take a liability together and agree privately that one of them will pay only if the other fails. The creditor is not a party to that private agreement. Each of the two stays fully liable to the creditor, even if the creditor knows of the arrangement.

Example. Hari and Gita sign one joint loan document for Rs 1 lakh with a cooperative bank. Privately they agree that Gita will pay only if Hari fails. The bank knows this. The bank can still sue Gita directly. This follows the Act's illustration to section 132, where a joint and several promissory note signed by A as surety for B does not stop C from suing A.

Liability: none where the guarantee was obtained by misrepresentation or concealment (sections 142 to 144)

Rule. A guarantee is invalid if the creditor obtained it by a misrepresentation about a material part of the transaction, made by him or with his knowledge and assent. It is also invalid if he obtained it by keeping silent about material circumstances. An invalid guarantee binds no one, so the surety owes nothing.

Section 144 deals with a related case. A person may give a guarantee on the condition that the creditor will not act on it until another person joins as co-surety. If that other person does not join, the guarantee is not valid.

Example. Anand employs Bala as a cashier. Bala has already failed to account for some of the money he collected. Anand asks Chandu to guarantee Bala's accounts and does not tell him about this. Bala later defaults. Chandu's guarantee is invalid. This is the Act's illustration (a) to section 143.

Right against the principal debtor: subrogation and indemnity (sections 140 and 145)

Rule. The surety pays first and recovers afterwards. A surety who pays all he is liable for steps into the creditor's shoes under section 140. This is subrogation . The surety can then proceed against the principal debtor with the creditor's rights.

Section 145 adds a second route. The principal debtor impliedly promises to indemnify the surety. The surety recovers sums he has rightfully paid, and not sums he paid wrongfully.

Example. Suresh is surety for Kiran's debt to Mohan. Mohan sues Suresh. Suresh defends on reasonable grounds but loses and pays the debt with costs. He recovers both the debt and the costs from Kiran. Had he defended with no reasonable ground, he could recover the debt but not the costs. This follows the Act's illustrations to section 145.

Right against the creditor: benefit of securities (section 141)

Rule. The surety is entitled to every security the creditor holds against the principal debtor when the guarantee is given. This is so even if the surety does not know about it. If the creditor loses it, or gives it up without the surety's consent, the surety is discharged to the extent of its value.

Only the security that exists when the guarantee is given counts. A further security that the creditor obtains later is not covered.

Example. Lakshmi lends her tenant Venkat Rs 2,000 on Ramesh's guarantee. She also holds a mortgage of Venkat's furniture for the debt. She cancels the mortgage. Venkat becomes insolvent and Lakshmi sues Ramesh. Ramesh is discharged to the value of the furniture. This is the Act's illustration (a) to section 141.

Right against co-sureties: sharing the loss (sections 138, 146 and 147)

Rule. Where several sureties guarantee the same debt, each must bear an equal share unless they agreed otherwise. If they are bound in different sums, they pay equally within the limits of those sums. So a surety who pays more than his share can claim the excess from the others.

If the creditor releases one co-surety, the others are not discharged (section 138). The released surety is also not freed from his responsibility to the other sureties.

Example. A, B and C are sureties to D for Rs 3,000 lent to E. E defaults. As between themselves they pay Rs 1,000 each. In the Act's illustration to section 147, sureties are bound in sums of Rs 10,000, Rs 20,000 and Rs 40,000, and the debtor defaults by Rs 40,000. A pays Rs 10,000, and B and C pay Rs 15,000 each.

The principal debtor defaults and the creditor demands payment. 2. The surety is liable at once (section 128). The creditor need not sue the principal debtor first. 3. The surety pays all he is liable for. 4. The surety takes over the creditor's rights (section 140) and has an implied indemnity (section 145). Sums rightfully paid can be recovered from the principal debtor. 5. Co-sureties share the loss (sections 146 and 147). A surety who paid more than his share recovers the excess from the others.

## Key Points to Remember
- Section 128: the surety's liability is coextensive with the principal debtor's, unless the contract says otherwise. The liability is immediate. The creditor need not exhaust remedies against the principal debtor first (Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad). Mere forbearance to sue does not discharge the surety (section 137). The surety's rights against the principal debtor begin on payment: section 140 (creditor's rights) and section 145 (implied indemnity). Section 141: the surety has the benefit of the securities held when the guarantee is given. A later security is not covered. Sections 146 and 147: co-sureties contribute equally, within the limits of their sums. Release of one co-surety does not discharge the others (section 138). A private arrangement between two joint promisors does not bind the creditor (section 132). A guarantee obtained by misrepresentation or concealment is invalid (sections 142 and 143).
