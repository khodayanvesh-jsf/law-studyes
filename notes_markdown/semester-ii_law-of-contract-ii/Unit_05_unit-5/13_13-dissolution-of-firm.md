# 13. Dissolution of firm
**Subject:** Law of Contracts – II (Semester II) | **Unit 5:** Unit 5
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-contract-ii/dissolution-of-firm/

## In One Minute
The dissolution of partnership between all the partners of a firm is called the "dissolution of the firm" (section 39). A firm may be dissolved by agreement (section 40), compulsorily (section 41), on the happening of certain contingencies (section 42), by notice in a partnership at will (section 43), or by the Court (section 44). After dissolution, partners remain liable to third parties until public notice is given (section 45). Every partner is entitled to have the firm's property applied in payment of its debts and the surplus distributed (section 46). The authority of partners continues only so far as needed to wind up (section 47). Accounts are settled by the rules in section 48. Sections 49 to 55 deal with joint and separate debts, personal profits, return of premium, fraud, use of the firm name, restraint of trade and goodwill.

## Meaning and Definition
Modes of dissolution (sections 40 to 44)

Rule. A firm may be dissolved in these five ways.

By agreement: with the consent of all the partners or in accordance with a contract between them (section 40). Compulsorily: by the adjudication of all the partners, or all but one, as insolvent; or by an event that makes the business, or carrying it on in partnership, unlawful. Illegality of one of several separate undertakings does not of itself dissolve the firm for the lawful ones (section 41). On contingencies, subject to contract: expiry of a fixed term; completion of the adventure or undertaking; death of a partner; adjudication of a partner as an insolvent (section 42). By notice in a partnership at will: any partner gives written notice to all the other partners. The firm is dissolved from the date in the notice, or if none is mentioned, from the date the notice is communicated (section 43). By the Court, at the suit of a partner, on the seven grounds in section 44(a) to (g): unsound mind of a partner; permanent incapacity of a partner other than the one suing; conduct prejudicial to the business; wilful or persistent breach of agreements or conduct making it not reasonably practicable to carry on in partnership; transfer of the whole interest to a third party or its being charged or sold; business cannot be carried on save at a loss; and any ground that makes it just and equitable.

Example. Anwar and Bhavna agree in writing to run a shop for five years. At the end of five years, the firm is dissolved by the expiry of the fixed term, subject to their contract (section 42(a)).

Liability after dissolution (section 45)

Rule. After dissolution, the partners remain liable to third parties for acts that would have been acts of the firm if done before dissolution, until public notice of the dissolution is given. The estate of a partner who dies or is adjudicated insolvent, and a partner who retired without being known to the third party to be a partner, are not liable for acts after the date they cease to be partners. Any partner may give the notice.

Winding up and continuing authority (sections 46 and 47)

Rule. Every partner or his representative is entitled to have the firm's property applied in paying its debts and liabilities, and the surplus distributed according to the partners' rights (section 46). The partners' authority to bind the firm, and their mutual rights and obligations, continue after dissolution only so far as necessary to wind up and to complete unfinished transactions. The firm is never bound by the acts of a partner adjudicated insolvent (section 47).

Settlement of accounts (sections 48 and 49)

Rule. Subject to the partners' agreement, section 48 sets the rules. Losses, including capital deficiencies, are paid first out of profits, next out of capital, and lastly by the partners individually in the profit-sharing proportions. The assets, including sums contributed by partners to make up capital deficiencies, are applied in this order: debts to third parties; each partner's advances, rateably; each partner's capital, rateably; the residue, divided in the profit-sharing proportions. Where there are joint debts of the firm and separate debts of a partner, the firm's property goes first to the firm's debts, with any surplus applied to the separate debts of each partner. A partner's separate property goes first to his separate debts and any surplus to the firm's debts (section 49).

Example. On dissolution, a firm's assets are Rs 5,00,000. It owes third parties Rs 2,00,000. Partner Asha advanced Rs 50,000 as a loan to the firm, and the capital contributed was Rs 2,00,000. In order: Rs 2,00,000 to third parties; Rs 50,000 to Asha as advances; then capital; then the residue in profit-sharing proportions (section 48(b)).

Personal profits, premium, fraud, firm name, restraint, goodwill (sections 50 to 55)

Rule. These sections do the following.

Section 50: subject to contract, a surviving partner or a deceased partner's representatives must account for personal profits (section 16(a)) from transactions after dissolution by death and before the affairs are wound up. Section 51: a partner who paid a premium for a fixed term is entitled to its repayment, or a reasonable part, if the firm is dissolved early otherwise than by a partner's death, unless the dissolution is mainly due to his misconduct or follows an agreement with no provision for return. Section 52: where a partnership contract is rescinded for fraud or misrepresentation, the party entitled to rescind has a lien on the surplus or assets for sums paid for a share and capital contributed, may rank as a creditor for payments towards the firm's debts, and is indemnified by the guilty partner or partners against all the firm's debts. Section 53: after dissolution, unless the contract says otherwise, each partner may restrain another from carrying on a similar business in the firm name, or from using the firm's property for his own benefit, until the affairs are wound up. A partner who has bought the goodwill may still use the firm name. Section 54: partners may agree that some or all will not carry on a similar business within a specified period or local limits, valid if reasonable, notwithstanding section 27 of the Contract Act. Section 55: goodwill is included in the assets and may be sold. After its sale a partner may carry on a competing business and advertise it, but, subject to agreement with the buyer, may not use the firm name, represent himself as carrying on the firm's business, or solicit its old customers. An agreement with the buyer not to carry on a similar business within specified limits is valid if reasonable.

How can a firm be dissolved? How to read this: start at the top. 1. Do all the partners consent, or does the contract provide for it? Dissolution by agreement (section 40). 2. Have all (or all but one) been adjudicated insolvent, or has the business become unlawful? Compulsory dissolution (section 41). 3. Has the term expired, the undertaking been completed, or a partner died or been adjudicated insolvent (subject to contract)? Dissolution on contingencies (section 42). 4. Is it a partnership at will? Any partner may dissolve by written notice to all (section 43). 5. Is there a ground in section 44(a) to (g)? A partner may sue and the Court may dissolve the firm.

## Key Points to Remember
- Dissolution of the firm: dissolution of partnership between all the partners (section 39). Five modes: agreement (40), compulsory (41), contingencies subject to contract (42), notice if at will (43), Court (44). Partners are liable to third parties until public notice (section 45). Authority continues only for winding up (section 47). Order of payment: third-party debts, advances, capital, residue (section 48(b)). Goodwill is an asset and may be sold (section 55).
