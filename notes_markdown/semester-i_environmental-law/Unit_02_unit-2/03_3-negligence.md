# 3. negligence
**Subject:** Environmental Law (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2

## In One Minute
Negligence is breach of a legal duty to take care that causes damage. A victim of pollution can use it when the polluter was careless. The victim must prove four things: duty, breach, causation and damage. The polluter can win by proving it took reasonable care.

## Legal Provision
- Negligence is a judge-made tort. No statute defines it for pollution cases. These sources apply.
- Law of torts: duty, breach, causation and damage, built through decided cases. Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932): the neighbour principle, the basis of the modern duty of care. Section 38, Specific Relief Act, 1963: a perpetual injunction may stop a continuing careless discharge.

## Meaning and Definition
Negligence is the breach of a legal duty to take care, which results in damage to the plaintiff. It is about carelessness, not about how direct the harm was. Many pollution harms come from careless operation or maintenance and not from a direct act.

The four elements of negligence How to read this: follow the boxes from top to bottom. A claim fails if any one box is missing. 1. Duty of care The defendant owed the plaintiff an obligation to take care. 2. Breach The defendant fell below the standard of a reasonable person. 3. Causation The breach caused the harm, and the harm is not too remote. 4. Damage The plaintiff suffered actual loss or injury.

## Key Points to Remember
- Negligence: duty, breach, causation, damage. Damage must be proved. Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932): neighbour principle. Standard of care is objective. Res ipsa loquitur allows an inference of negligence. Reasonable care defeats the claim. This is the key difference from strict liability.
