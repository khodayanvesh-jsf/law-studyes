# 3. Schools of Hindu Law
**Subject:** Family Law – I (Hindu Law) (Semester I) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/family-law-i/schools-of-hindu-law/

## In One Minute
A school of Hindu law is a body of rules built on one leading commentary and followed in one part of India. There are only two schools: the Mitakshara and the Dayabhaga . The Dayabhaga prevails in Bengal (and Assam). The Mitakshara prevails in the rest of India. The Mitakshara school has four sub-schools: Benares, Mithila, Maharashtra or Bombay, and Dravida or Madras. They all accept the Mitakshara as supreme and differ only in details, mainly of adoption and inheritance. The two schools differ mainly on inheritance and the joint family. Today the Hindu Acts apply to both schools alike, so the difference matters mainly for joint family property. Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act speaks only of a family "governed by the Mitakshara law". Exams ask why the schools arose, the two schools and their regions, the four sub-schools with their regions and leading works, and what the schools mean today ( Vineeta Sharma , Surjit Lal Chhabda ).

## Legal Provision
- No Act creates or lists the schools. They come from the commentaries and the courts. The Acts refer to them in a few places.
- Section 6(1), Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (as substituted in 2005). On and from the commencement of the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 (39 of 2005), in a Joint Hindu family governed by the Mitakshara law, the daughter of a coparcener shall,― (a) by birth become a coparcener in her own right the same manner as the son; (b) have the same rights in the coparcenery property as she would have had if she had been a son; (c) be subject to the same liabilities in respect of the said coparcenery property as that of a son, and any reference to a Hindu Mitakshara coparcener shall be deemed to include a reference to a daughter of a coparcener: ...
- Section 4(1), Hindu Succession Act, 1956. Overriding effect of Act. Save as otherwise expressly provided in this Act,― (a) any text, rule or interpretation of Hindu law or any custom or usage as part of that law in force immediately before the commencement of this Act shall cease to have effect with respect to any matter for which provision is made in this Act; (b) any other law in force immediately before the commencement of this Act shall cease to apply to Hindus in so far as it is inconsistent with any of the provisions contained in this Act.
- Section 3(1), Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act, 1937 (a pre-1956 Act, quoted to show how statutes used the schools). When a Hindu governed by the Dayabhaga School of Hindu Law dies intestate leaving any property, and when a Hindu governed by any other school of Hindu law or by customary law dies intestate leaving separate property, his widow ... shall ... be entitled ... to the same share as a son: ...
- In simple words
- Section 6(1): the daughter's birthright as a coparcener applies only in a joint Hindu family "governed by the Mitakshara law". A Dayabhaga family is outside these words. Section 4(1): on every matter the Succession Act covers, the old texts and interpretations of each school stop having effect. A school's rules survive only on matters the Act does not cover. The other three Acts have the same clause (Hindu Marriage Act s. 4; Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act s. 4; Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act s. 5). Section 3(a), Hindu Marriage Act: a custom that is ancient, certain, reasonable and not against public policy has the force of law. A local rule of a sub-school can therefore survive as custom where the Act saves custom. 1937 Act: before codification, statutes named the schools and gave different rules to a Dayabhaga Hindu and to a Hindu of "any other school". On matters the 1956 Act covers, such earlier law ceased to apply under Section 4(1)(b).

## Meaning and Definition
A school of Hindu law is a system of rules built on one authoritative commentary and followed in a particular region. The schools share the same ancient sources, the Smritis. They differ because each region accepted a different commentator's reading of those texts. See Diagram 1 .

Commentaries gave rise to two schools: the Mitakshara (Vijnaneswara, commentary on Yajnavalkya, all India except Bengal) and the Dayabhaga (Jimutavahana, digest, Bengal and Assam). The Mitakshara has four sub-schools: Benares (Northern India), Mithila (Tirhoot and north Bihar), Maharashtra or Bombay (Western India), Dravida or Madras (Southern India).

The question this topic answers. Which body of Hindu law governs this family? The answer still matters for joint family property and coparcenary rights, and for any matter the Acts do not cover.

Why the law has this rule. The Smritis sometimes conflicted. Commentators settled the conflicts in different ways. A commentator's view accepted in one region and rejected in another became the law of the region that accepted it. Courts then followed what each region had accepted.

## Key Points to Remember
- The Smritis are common to all schools; the schools arose from commentaries accepted in different regions. Only two schools: Mitakshara (Vijnaneswara, commentary on Yajnavalkya) and Dayabhaga (Jimutavahana, digest of all Codes). Dayabhaga: Bengal and Assam. Mitakshara: the rest of India. The schools differ mainly on inheritance and the joint family system. Four Mitakshara sub-schools: Benares, Mithila, Maharashtra or Bombay, Dravida or Madras. Sub-school works supplement the Mitakshara; first ask what the Mitakshara says. The Mayukha overrules the Mitakshara only in Gujarat, the Island of Bombay and the North Konkan. The Hindu Acts apply to both schools alike; Section 6 of the Succession Act speaks only of the Mitakshara.
