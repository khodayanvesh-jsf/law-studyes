# 4. Strict Liability & Absolute Liability
**Subject:** Environmental Law (Semester 1) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/environmental-law/strict-liability-and-absolute-liability/

## In One Minute
Strict and absolute liability make a person pay for harm from a dangerous thing or activity, even if they took care. The victim does not have to prove fault. Strict liability comes from Rylands v. Fletcher (1868) and has exceptions. Absolute liability comes from M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (the Oleum case, 1986) and has none. This is the most examined topic in the group.

## Legal Provision
- Both rules are judge-made. The law comes from these sources.
- Rylands v. Fletcher (1868), House of Lords: a person who brings and keeps on land anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it in at his peril . He is answerable for the natural consequence of its escape. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1986), Supreme Court: an enterprise in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. It is liable for harm from an accident in that activity, without the Rylands exceptions. Article 21, Constitution of India: the right to life. The Supreme Court allowed compensation claims in a writ petition under Article 32 because a hazardous industry threatens this right. Section 17(3), National Green Tribunal Act, 2010: in an accident case the Tribunal applies the principle of no fault.

## Meaning and Definition
Strict liability is liability without proof of fault. The defendant is liable for the escape of a dangerous thing brought on to land for a non-natural use, subject to exceptions.

Absolute liability is the Indian rule for hazardous or inherently dangerous enterprises. Liability follows the harm. Taking all reasonable care is no answer, and no exception applies.

## Key Points to Remember
- Strict liability: Rylands v. Fletcher (1868). Non-natural use plus escape. Exceptions apply. Absolute liability: M.C. Mehta (Oleum, 1986). Hazardous activity plus harm. No exceptions. Exceptions to strict liability: act of God, act of a stranger, plaintiff's default, statutory authority, consent. Compensation under absolute liability is matched to the size and capacity of the enterprise. The Bichhri case (1996) held the Oleum rule binding and applied the polluter pays principle. In an exam, always name both cases together.
