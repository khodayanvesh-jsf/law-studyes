# 8. Role of Indian Judiciary in the evolution of environmental jurisprudence
**Subject:** Environmental Law (Semester 1) | **Unit 4:** Unit 4
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/environmental-law/role-of-indian-judiciary-in-the-evolution-of-environmental-jurisprudence/

## In One Minute
Much of Indian environmental law was built by the Supreme Court, case by case. The Court read a right to a clean environment into Article 21. It relaxed who may sue, created new principles, and supervised the government for years. The key doctrines are absolute liability, the precautionary principle, the polluter pays principle, sustainable development and the public trust doctrine. Parliament later put some of them into statute, such as section 20 of the National Green Tribunal Act.

## Legal Provision
- Article 21: protection of life and personal liberty, read to include a healthy environment. Articles 32 and 226: the writ powers of the Supreme Court and the High Courts. Articles 48A and 51A(g): the State's directive and the citizen's duty on the environment, used as support. Article 142: the Supreme Court's power to do complete justice, used for directions in Hanuman Laxman Aroskar. Section 20, NGT Act, 2010: the Tribunal must apply sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle.

## Meaning and Definition
Environmental jurisprudence is the body of rules and principles that courts have developed to protect the environment. In India the courts filled gaps where statutes were silent or weakly enforced. They did so mainly through writ petitions.

## Key Points to Remember
- Oleum (decided December 1986): absolute liability, no exceptions. Vellore (1996): precautionary and polluter pays principles. Kamal Nath (1996): public trust doctrine. Narmada (2000): 2:1, sustainable development. Section 20, NGT Act: principles now statutory.
