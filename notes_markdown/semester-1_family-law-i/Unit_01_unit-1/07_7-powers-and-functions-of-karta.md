# 7. Powers and Functions of Karta
**Subject:** Family Law – I (Hindu Law) (Semester 1) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/family-law-i/powers-and-functions-of-karta/

## In One Minute
The Karta manages the joint family and its property. His powers are wide in day-to-day matters: he controls income and spending, runs the family business, contracts debts for family purposes, makes contracts, gives receipts, compromises and refers disputes to arbitration, and sues and is sued for the family. His power to sell or mortgage family property is limited. The Privy Council in Brij Narain v. Mangla Prasad (1923) held that a managing coparcener "cannot alienate or burden the estate qua manager except for purposes of necessity". Mulla adds a second ground: benefit of the estate. An alienation outside these grounds is voidable at the option of the other coparceners. A father who is Karta has an extra power. He may, by a debt not incurred for an immoral purpose, lay the estate open to execution for that debt. A mortgage binds the estate only if it pays off an antecedent debt , antecedent "in fact as well as in time". His functions are the duties that go with the office: maintaining the members, meeting marriage and ceremony expenses, keeping the property, and accounting when the family divides. Exams ask for the Brij Narain propositions, the meaning of legal necessity , and the father's special power.

## Legal Provision
- The Karta 's powers are not in an Act. They come from Mitakshara law and court decisions. Three provisions of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 bear on them.
- Section 6(1), proviso (as substituted in 2005). Provided that nothing contained in this sub-section shall affect or invalidate any disposition or alienation including any partition or testamentary disposition of property which had taken place before the 20th day of December, 2004.
- Section 6(4). After the commencement of the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 (39 of 2005), no court shall recognise any right to proceed against a son, grandson or great-grandson for the recovery of any debt due from his father, grandfather or great-grandfather solely on the ground of the pious obligation under the Hindu law, of such son, grandson or great-grandson to discharge any such debt: Provided that in the case of any debt contracted before the commencement of the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 (39 of 2005), nothing contained in this sub-section shall affect― (a) the right of any creditor to proceed against the son, grandson or great-grandson, as the case may be; or (b) any alienation made in respect of or in satisfaction of, any such debt, and any such right or alienation shall be enforceable under the rule of pious obligation in the same manner and to the same extent as it would have been enforceable as if the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 (39 of 2005) had not been enacted. Explanation.―For the purposes of clause (a), the expression "son", "grandson" or "great-grandson" shall be deemed to refer to the son, grandson or great-grandson, as the case may be, who was born or adopted prior to the commencement of the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 (39 of 2005).
- Section 30, Explanation. The interest of a male Hindu in a Mitakshara coparcenary property or the interest of a member of a tarwad, tavazhi, illom, kutumba or kavaru in the property of the tarwad, tavazhi, illom, kutumba or kavaru shall, notwithstanding anything contained in this Act or in any other law for the time being in force, be deemed to be property capable of being disposed of by him or by her within the meaning of this section.
- In simple words
- Section 6(1) makes a daughter a coparcener by birth, so she can be Karta and exercise all the powers in this note ( Sujata Sharma v. Manu Gupta , Delhi High Court, 2015). The proviso to Section 6(1) protects any sale, mortgage, gift, partition or will made before 20 December 2004. A Karta's earlier valid alienation therefore stands against a daughter's claim. Section 6(4) ends the pious obligation for debts taken after 9 September 2005 (the commencement of the 2005 Act). For debts taken before that date, a creditor's rights against sons, grandsons and great-grandsons born or adopted before that date are saved. Vineeta Sharma (para 61) adds that a daughter is equally bound by the pious obligation in the same way as a son. The Section 30 Explanation lets a coparcener dispose of his own undivided interest by will. That is a power of every coparcener over his own interest, not a power of the Karta over the whole property.

## Meaning and Definition
The Karta's powers are the legal capacities that let him act for the joint family as its manager and bind the other members. His functions are the duties that come with the office.

Two ideas fix the limits. First, no coparcener can claim a precise share while the property is undivided, because the interest "is fluctuating" and "increases and diminishes by death and birth in the family" ( Vineeta Sharma , para 26). So the Karta, too, holds no separate share he can deal with as his own. Second, one of the incidents of a coparcenary is "that there can be no alienation of the property without the concurrence of the other coparceners unless it be for legal necessity " ( Vineeta Sharma , para 30, quoting Controller of Estate Duty v. Alladi Kuppuswamy on Ghamandi Ram ).

Mulla (section 236) puts it this way: the manager has no larger interest than any other member; "The only respect in which he has a superior right is that he has a power of disposition for causes recognised as just and proper under Hindu law of the whole family property, including the interest of the junior members." ## Key Points to Remember
- The Karta manages the family and represents it ( Tribhovandas ). He controls income; members who dislike his spending can only seek partition (Mulla s. 237). His debts for family purposes bind the family property, not other members' separate property (Mulla s. 240). He may contract, give receipts, compromise, refer to arbitration and give discharges, but cannot give up a family debt (Mulla ss. 241–250). He cannot alienate or burden the estate except for necessity ( Brij Narain , para 17) or benefit of the estate (Mulla s. 242); otherwise the alienation is voidable. A father may lay the estate open to execution for his debt not for an immoral purpose; a mortgage binds only for an antecedent debt, antecedent in fact as well as in time. Section 6(4): no recovery from sons solely on pious obligation for debts after 9 September 2005. A sole surviving coparcener may dispose of the property; the alienation stands after a son's birth ( Vineeta Sharma , para 28).
