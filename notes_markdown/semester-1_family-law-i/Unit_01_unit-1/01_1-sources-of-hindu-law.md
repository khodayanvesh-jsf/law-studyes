# 1. Sources of Hindu Law
**Subject:** Family Law – I (Hindu Law) (Semester 1) | **Unit 1:** Unit 1
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/family-law-i/sources-of-hindu-law/

## In One Minute
The sources of Hindu law are the places its rules come from. The old sources are Shruti (the Vedas), Smriti (the ancient law books), the commentaries and digests written on the Smritis, and custom . The modern sources are Acts of Parliament, court decisions, and justice, equity and good conscience. Today the order is reversed. The Acts of 1955 and 1956 come first. Each Act says that the old texts and customs stop having effect on any matter the Act covers, unless the Act itself keeps them. Custom survives only where an Act saves it, for example customary marriage rites (Section 7) and customary divorce (Section 29(2) of the Hindu Marriage Act). Exams ask for the list of sources, the meaning of each, the test for a valid custom, and the rule that clear proof of usage outweighs the written text. Write both layers, old and modern, and end with how they rank today.

## Legal Provision
- No single section lists the sources. These provisions decide what a custom is and how the sources rank.
- Section 3(a), Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. the expressions "custom" and "usage" signify any rule which, having been continuously and uniformly observed for a long time, has obtained the force of law among Hindus in any local area, tribe, community, group or family: Provided that the rule is certain and not unreasonable or opposed to public policy; and Provided further that in the case of a rule applicable only to a family it has not been discontinued by the family;
- Section 4, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. Overriding effect of Act. Save as otherwise expressly provided in this Act,— (a) any text rule or interpretation of Hindu law or any custom or usage as part of that law in force immediately before the commencement of this Act shall cease to have effect with respect to any matter for which provision is made in this Act; (b) any other law in force immediately before the commencement of this Act shall cease to have effect in so far as it is inconsistent with any of the provisions contained in this Act.
- Section 29(2), Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. Nothing contained in this Act shall be deemed to affect any right recognised by custom or conferred by any special enactment to obtain the dissolution of a Hindu marriage, whether solemnized before or after the commencement of this Act.
- Article 13(3)(a), Constitution. "law" includes any Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having in the territory of India the force of law;
- Article 141, Constitution. The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India.
- In simple words
- Section 3(a): a custom is a rule that has been followed continuously and in the same way for a long time, so that it has become law for Hindus of a local area, tribe, community, group or family. It must be certain, and not unreasonable or against public policy. A family custom must not have been given up by the family. Section 3(1)(d) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 gives the same definition. Section 4: on any matter the Act deals with, the old texts, their interpretation and any custom stop having effect. Any other earlier law stops having effect where it clashes with the Act. Section 4 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 and of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, and Section 5 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, say the same thing. Both limbs start with "Save as otherwise expressly provided in this Act", so an express saving in the Act survives. Section 29(2): the Act does not take away a right, recognised by custom or given by a special law, to obtain a divorce. Article 13(3)(a): custom having the force of law is "law" for Part III, so it must respect the fundamental rights. Article 141: what the Supreme Court declares as law binds every court in India.

## Meaning and Definition
A source of law is the origin of a legal rule: the place a judge looks to find it. Hindu law grew over many centuries, so its sources come in two layers. See Diagram 1 .

Ancient sources: Shruti, Smriti, commentaries and digests, and custom. Mulla (Principles of Hindu Law, 14th ed., section 8) names the three main sources of Hindu law as the Sruti, the Smriti and custom. For practical purposes he divides them into the original texts, the commentaries and digests, and custom. Modern sources: legislation, judicial decisions, and justice, equity and good conscience . Mulla (section 8) says these must be added "for all practical purposes" to the main sources.

The question this topic answers. Where does a court find a rule of Hindu law, and which source wins when two of them differ?

Why it matters. The old texts were written for a very different society. The Acts of 1955 and 1956 changed much of the law. A student must know which source still governs a matter, which one has been displaced, and where custom is still allowed.

## Key Points to Remember
- Ancient sources: Shruti, Smriti, commentaries and digests, custom. Modern sources: legislation, precedent, justice, equity and good conscience. Shruti = that which was heard; Smriti = that which was remembered. Three principal Smritis: Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada. Mitakshara: commentary on Yajnavalkya by Vijnaneswara. Dayabhaga: digest of all Codes by Jimutavahana. "Clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law" ( Collector of Madura ). Section 3(a): custom must be continuous, uniform, long, certain, reasonable and not against public policy; a family custom must not be discontinued. Section 4 of each Act ends old text and custom on matters the Act covers, unless the Act expressly saves them. Custom saved by the Hindu Marriage Act: Sections 5(iv), 5(v), 7(1), 29(2). Hindu law today, apart from codified branches, is "Judge-made law" ( Surjit Lal Chhabda ).
