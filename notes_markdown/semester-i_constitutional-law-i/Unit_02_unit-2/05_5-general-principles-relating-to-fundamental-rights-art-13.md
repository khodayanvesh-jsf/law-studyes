# 5. General Principles relating to Fundamental Rights(Art.13)
**Subject:** Constitutional Law – I (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/constitutional-law-i/general-principles-relating-to-fundamental-rights-art-13/

## In One Minute
Part III (Articles 12 to 35) lists the Fundamental Rights. Article 13 gives them force: a law that conflicts with them is void to the extent of the conflict. Courts have also built rules around it, such as severability, eclipse and waiver. Exams ask for Article 13(1) to (4), the doctrine of eclipse, severability, waiver and the Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi shift. Keep eclipse and severability apart.

## Legal Provision
- Article 13(1): laws in force in India immediately before the Constitution began are void, to the extent they are inconsistent with Part III. Article 13(2): the State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights in Part III. A law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contravention. Article 13(3): "law" includes any ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law. "Laws in force" includes laws passed before commencement that are not repealed, even if not then in operation. Article 13(4): nothing in Article 13 applies to an amendment made under Article 368. It was inserted by the Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971, section 2, in force from 5 November 1971. Article 368(3) says the same: nothing in Article 13 applies to any amendment made under Article 368. Article 32: the right to move the Supreme Court to enforce Part III is guaranteed. Articles 33 to 35: Parliament may restrict or abrogate rights for the armed forces and similar forces (Article 33). It may indemnify acts done under martial law (Article 34). It alone may make laws punishing offences under Part III (Article 35). Article 300A: no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. Article 31 was omitted by the 44th Amendment (in force from 20 June 1979).

## Meaning and Definition
Fundamental Rights are the rights guaranteed in Part III. They are fundamental for three reasons. They are entrenched in the Constitution, so an ordinary law cannot take them away. They can be enforced directly in the Supreme Court (Article 32) and the High Courts (Article 226). And any law that conflicts with them is void (Article 13).

Part III groups them as follows.

Right to equality: Articles 14 to 18. Right to freedom: Articles 19 to 22. Right against exploitation: Articles 23 and 24. Right to freedom of religion: Articles 25 to 28. Cultural and educational rights: Articles 29 and 30. Right to constitutional remedies: Article 32.

The right to property is no longer a Fundamental Right. The 44th Amendment omitted Article 31 and Article 19(1)(f), and inserted Article 300A. Property is now a legal right, not a right under Part III.

Is the measure void under Article 13? How to read this: go down the boxes in order. A "No" answer sends you to the result beside the box. 1. Is it a "law" under Article 13(3), such as an Act, ordinance, rule, notification, custom or usage? Article 13(3) No: Article 13 does not apply. A constitutional amendment under Article 368 is outside it (Article 13(4)) and is tested only on the basic structure. 2. Does it take away or abridge a Part III right, or (for an earlier law) conflict with Part III? Article 13(1), 13(2) No: Article 13 does not strike it down. 3. Can the bad part be separated from the rest? doctrine of severability No: the whole law falls. Yes: only the inconsistent part is void. A pre-Constitution law is dormant and revives if the conflict is later removed. A law made after commencement is void from the start.

## Key Points to Remember
- Part III covers Articles 12 to 35; the right to property left Part III in 1979 (Article 300A). Article 13(1): earlier laws void to the extent of inconsistency. Article 13(2): later laws void. Article 13(4) and Article 368(3): amendments are outside Article 13, but not outside the basic structure. Eclipse (Bhikaji): only earlier laws; they revive. Severability (Gopalan): only the bad part falls. Waiver (Basheshar Nath): rights cannot ordinarily be waived. Maneka Gandhi (1978): Articles 14, 19 and 21 read together; fair procedure.
