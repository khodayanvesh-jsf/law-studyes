# 7. Doctrine of Judicial Review
**Subject:** Constitutional Law – I (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/constitutional-law-i/doctrine-of-judicial-review/

## In One Minute
Judicial review is the power of courts to test a law or an executive act against the Constitution and set it aside if it conflicts. In India the power is written into the Constitution. It reaches ordinary laws, executive action and even constitutional amendments. Exams ask for the Articles behind it, what can be reviewed, and three cases: Minerva Mills, I.R. Coelho and L. Chandra Kumar. Learn why the power cannot be taken away.

## Legal Provision
- Article 13: a law inconsistent with Part III is void to the extent of the inconsistency. Article 13(4) and Article 368(3) keep amendments outside it. Article 32: the right to move the Supreme Court is guaranteed. The Court can issue directions, orders or writs, including habeas corpus , mandamus , prohibition , quo warranto and certiorari . Article 32 is itself in Part III. Article 226: every High Court can issue writs to any person or authority within its territories, for Part III rights "and for any other purpose". Article 227: every High Court has superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territories, except those under the Armed Forces laws. Article 131: original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in disputes between the Government of India and States, or between States. Article 136: special leave to appeal from any court or tribunal. Articles 245 and 246: the legislative reach of Parliament and State Legislatures, and the three Lists of the Seventh Schedule. Article 368(4) and (5): inserted by the 42nd Amendment, declared invalid in Minerva Mills. The printed text still shows them.

## Meaning and Definition
Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine whether a law or executive act is valid under the Constitution and to declare it void if it is not. It rests on the supremacy of the Constitution. An organ of government that goes beyond the Constitution acts without authority to that extent.

In the United States, the Supreme Court asserted this power in Marbury v. Madison (1803), although the American text does not state it in words. India differs, because Articles 13, 32, 131, 136, 226, 227, 245 and 246 give the power expressly.

How a court reviews a law How to read this: start at the top. Each box must be answered "yes" to move down. Red text shows where the law is struck down. 1. Was the law made within the legislature's competence? (Articles 245, 246, Seventh Schedule) No: the law is void for want of competence. 2. Does it respect Part III? (Article 13) No: it is void to the extent of the conflict, subject to severability. 3. Does it respect other limits in the Constitution? No: it is void to that extent. 4. If it is a constitutional amendment: does it leave the basic structure intact? No: the amendment is struck down (Kesavananda Bharati, Minerva Mills). 5. Result The law stands. Courts do not ordinarily test its wisdom.

## Key Points to Remember
- Judicial review: courts test laws and executive acts against the Constitution. Express textual basis: Articles 13, 32, 226, 227, 131, 136, 245, 246. Reviewable: legislation, executive action, constitutional amendments (basic structure only). Minerva Mills (1980): Article 368(4) and (5) void; a limited amending power is a basic feature. I.R. Coelho (2007): Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 can be tested on the basic structure. L. Chandra Kumar (1997): High Court review under Articles 226 and 227 cannot be excluded; tribunals are supplemental. Article 32 is a Fundamental Right; Article 226 is a High Court power.
