# 1. Directive Principles of State Policy
**Subject:** Constitutional Law – I (Semester 1) | **Unit 5:** Unit 5
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/constitutional-law-i/directive-principles-of-state-policy/

## In One Minute
Part IV of the Constitution, Articles 36 to 51, lists goals that every government must work towards: justice, a living wage, education, a clean environment and peace. Article 37 says no court can enforce them. It also says they are fundamental in the governance of the country. Exams ask for the meaning of Article 37, the difference from Fundamental Rights, and the Champakam rule that a Fundamental Right wins in a direct clash. A problem question often asks whether a court can order the State to act on one of these goals.

## Legal Provision
- Article 36: in Part IV, "the State" has the same meaning as in Part III. So it covers the Union and State Governments, Parliament, the State Legislatures and local and other authorities. Article 37: the provisions of Part IV are not enforceable by any court. The principles are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country. It is the duty of the State to apply them in making laws. Article 38(1): the State shall strive to promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order in which social, economic and political justice informs all national institutions. Article 38(2): the State shall strive to minimise inequalities in income and to eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities. The 44th Amendment (20 June 1979) added this clause. Article 39: the State shall direct its policy towards six goals, (a) to (f). They include an adequate means of livelihood, distribution of material resources for the common good, and equal pay for equal work. Articles 40 to 51: the remaining principles, from village panchayats (Article 40) to international peace (Article 51). Articles 39A, 43A, 43B and 48A were added later by amendments. Article 15(4): the First Amendment (18 June 1951) allows special provision for socially and educationally backward classes and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. It followed Champakam.

## Meaning and Definition
The Directive Principles of State Policy are the instructions in Part IV that tell the legislature and the executive what goals to aim at. They are not rights that a person can claim. They are a standing instruction to every government. The Constitution calls them non-justiciable , which means a court cannot enforce them by itself.

Part III protects individuals against the State. Part IV asks the State to build a fair society. Together they aim at a welfare state that keeps both liberty and social justice.

Can a court enforce this claim? How to read this: go down the boxes in order. A "No" answer sends you to the result beside the box. 1. Does the claim rest only on a provision of Part IV? Article 37 No: it rests on a Fundamental Right or a statute, so a court can enforce it. 2. Has the legislature made a law that gives effect to the principle? Article 37, last words No: the court cannot order the State to make one. Yes to both: the court enforces the statute, and may read the principle into the Fundamental Right.

## Key Points to Remember
- Part IV, Articles 36 to 51, holds the Directive Principles of State Policy. Article 37: not enforceable by any court, yet fundamental in governance, and the State must apply them in making laws. Article 36 gives "State" the same meaning as in Part III. Champakam (1951): the Directive Principles run subsidiary to Fundamental Rights. The First Amendment added Article 15(4) after Champakam. Minerva Mills (1980): Parts III and IV together form the conscience of the Constitution.
