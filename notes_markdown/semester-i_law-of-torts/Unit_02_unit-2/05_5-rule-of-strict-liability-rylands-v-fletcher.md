# 5. Rule of Strict Liability (Rylands v. Fletcher)
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/rule-of-strict-liability/

## In One Minute
Under the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher, a person who brings and keeps something dangerous on his land for his own purposes must keep it in at his own risk. If it escapes and causes damage, he is liable even if he was not careless. This is strict liability. It has four essentials and several defences. In exams it is a long answer, often with a problem on a reservoir, a gas leak or a fire. The Indian rule of absolute liability was framed in reaction to it.

## Legal Provision
- No statute states the rule. It is a rule of common law, from the House of Lords in 1868. Article 21, Constitution of India: M.C. Mehta (1987) said the Rylands rule cannot give a standard of liability consistent with "the constitutional norms". Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991 and National Green Tribunal Act, 2010: statutes that now give no-fault relief for accidents. The 1991 Act covers hazardous substances (section 3). The Tribunal Act covers accidents under the Acts in its Schedule I (section 17). Section 164, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: statutory no-fault compensation of fixed amounts. Section 140, the earlier no-fault provision, was omitted from 1 April 2022. This kind of liability is separate from the rule, as Kaushnuma Begum explains. Seven defences: the Supreme Court listed them in Kaushnuma Begum (2001), quoting Winfield.

## Meaning and Definition
The rule was stated by Blackburn J in the Court of Exchequer Chamber in 1866 and affirmed by the House of Lords in 1868 ((1868) LR 3 HL 330). It says that the person who for his own purposes brings on his land and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it in at his peril. If he does not, he is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape.

In the House of Lords, Lord Cairns added a condition: the land must be put to a "non-natural use". The rule is strict liability because the claimant does not have to prove negligence.

The facts of Rylands v. Fletcher

Mill owners built a reservoir on their land through an engineer and contractors. Old, filled-up coal shafts lay under the site, and nobody knew of them. When the reservoir was filled, the water broke through the shafts and flooded the neighbour's mine. The owners were held liable although they took no part in the work and knew nothing of the shafts. The engineer and contractor had not taken reasonable care.

The case went through three courts. The Court of Exchequer held that the claimant had no cause of action . The Court of Exchequer Chamber unanimously reversed this and awarded damages. The House of Lords affirmed that decision and dismissed the appeal with costs (Lord Cairns and Lord Cranworth).

## Key Points to Remember
- Rule: bring and keep a dangerous thing on your land for your own purposes, and keep it in at your peril. Essentials: dangerous thing, non-natural use, escape, damage. No proof of negligence is needed. Seven defences: consent, common benefit, act of a stranger, statutory authority, act of God, claimant's default, remoteness. The House of Lords requires foreseeability of the harm (Cambridge Water). Kaushnuma Begum applied the rule to motor accidents. India: M.C. Mehta replaced it, for hazardous industry, with absolute liability.
