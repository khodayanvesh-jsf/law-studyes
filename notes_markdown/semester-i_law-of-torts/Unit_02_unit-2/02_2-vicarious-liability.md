# 2. Vicarious Liability
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/vicarious-liability/

## In One Minute
Vicarious liability makes one person answer for the tort of another, although he did nothing wrong himself. The usual case is an employer who must pay for a tort of his employee committed in the course of employment. It rests on the relationship, not on the employer's fault. Two things must be shown: a relationship such as employer and employee, and a tort committed in the course of employment. In exams, this is asked as a long answer, often with a problem on a driver or a servant.

## Legal Provision
- The general rule is judge-made; the provisions below deal with particular cases. It is a common law rule. The Supreme Court applied it in Pushpabai Purshottam Udeshi (1977) and in State of Rajasthan v. Vidyawati (1962). Sections 25 and 26, Indian Partnership Act, 1932. Every partner is liable , jointly with the other partners and also severally , for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner (section 25). If a partner's wrongful act or omission causes loss or injury to a third party, the firm is liable to the same extent as the partner. The act must be done in the ordinary course of the firm's business or with the authority of his partners (section 26). Article 300(1), Constitution of India. The Union sues and is sued as the Union of India, and a State as the State. They may sue or be sued in the like cases as the Dominion of India and the Provinces or Indian States before the Constitution, subject to any law made by Parliament or the State Legislature. The Supreme Court used this Article in Vidyawati. Section 164, Motor Vehicles Act, 1988. The owner or the authorised insurer must pay Rs 5 lakh for death and two and a half lakh rupees for grievous hurt caused by an accident arising out of the use of a motor vehicle (section 164(1)). The claimant need not plead or prove any wrongful act, neglect or default of the owner, the vehicle or any other person (section 164(2)). This is a no-fault payment, not vicarious liability, but it falls on the owner or insurer.

## Meaning and Definition
Vicarious liability is the liability of one person for the tort of another because of a relationship between them. Lord Steyn defined it in Lister v. Hesley Hall (para 14) as "legal responsibility imposed on an employer, although he is himself free from blame, for a tort committed by his employee in the course of his employment". The wrongdoer is called the tortfeasor .

Two elements must be shown. First, a relationship between the two persons that makes it proper for the law to make one pay for the fault of the other. Second, a connection between that relationship and the tortfeasor's wrongdoing (Barclays Bank v. Various Claimants, para 1; Cox v. Ministry of Justice, para 2).

The employee does not escape. In Young v. Edward Box & Co., which the Supreme Court applied in Pushpabai, Lord Denning asked two questions. First, is the servant liable? Second, must the employer shoulder the servant's liability? In Vidyawati, the widow and daughter of the dead man sued both the driver and the State.

The maxim qui facit per alium facit per se (he who acts through another acts himself) and the maxim respondeat superior (let the superior answer) are the old names for the rule. Lord Reid said that respondeat superior "merely states the rule baldly in two words" and that the other maxim "merely gives a fictional explanation of it" (quoted in Lister, para 35). The maxims name the rule but do not explain it.

Why the law imposes it

A compromise. Fleming called the rule a compromise between two policies. One is the interest in giving an innocent victim a claim against a defendant who can pay. The other is a hesitation to put an undue burden on business (Lister, para 14). Five reasons usually given. Lord Phillips listed them in the Christian Brothers case, and Lord Reed set them out in Cox (para 19). The employer is more likely to be able to compensate the victim and can be expected to have insured. The tort was committed through activity done on behalf of the employer. The activity is likely to be part of the employer's business. By employing the person for the activity, the employer created the risk of the tort. The employee was, to a greater or lesser degree, under the employer's control. Wealth and insurance alone are not enough. Lord Reed said that the mere possession of wealth is not a ground for liability. Employers insure because they are liable. They are not liable because they have insured (Cox, para 20). Control means what, not how. The ability to direct how a person does his work no longer has the importance it once had. Control now means that the defendant can direct what the person does. Without even that degree of control, vicarious liability is unlikely (Cox, para 21). Risk of the business. The essential idea is that a defendant should be liable for torts that may fairly be seen as risks of his business activities (Cox, para 23). Lord Nicholls said that "carrying on a business enterprise necessarily involves risks to others" (quoted in Cox, para 23). A cautious view. Lord Clyde warned that the old maxims do not help in analysis. He quoted Lord Cooper, who called the rule "a rule of expediency, imperfectly defined", and Lord Pearce, who said it grew from "social convenience and rough justice" (Lister, paras 34, 35).

## Key Points to Remember
- Vicarious liability needs no fault of the employer. It rests on the relationship. Conditions: employer-employee (or equivalent) relationship, and a tort in the course of employment. The Salmond test has two limbs: an authorised act, or an unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act. Lister adds the close connection test for intentional wrongs. A prohibition on the way of doing the job does not free the employer (Rose v. Plenty). A frolic of his own does. No single test decides who is an employee (Sushilaben, para 24). The employer of an independent contractor is in general not liable (Barclays, para 7). Partners are jointly and severally liable (sections 25 and 26, Partnership Act, 1932). The State is liable for torts of its servants outside sovereign powers (Vidyawati) but not for those in the exercise of sovereign powers (Kasturi Lal).
