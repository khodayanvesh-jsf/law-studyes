# 3. Liability of the State for Torts – Defense of Sovereign Immunity
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/liability-of-the-state-for-torts/

## In One Minute
The State can be sued in tort. Article 300 of the Constitution says so, in the same cases as before the Constitution. The old English rule that "the King can do no wrong" gave the State immunity for acts done in the exercise of sovereign power . The Supreme Court has cut back that immunity. The State is liable for non-sovereign acts, and it must pay compensation for violation of fundamental rights. In exams, expect the sovereign and non-sovereign test, the cases from P. and O. Steam Navigation to Chandrima Das, and foreign sovereigns and act of state. The Law Commission asked for a statute in 1956. The Government (Liability in Torts) Bill, 1967 did not become law.

## Legal Provision
- Article 300(1), Constitution of India: the Government of India may sue or be sued in the name of the Union of India, and a State in the name of the State. They may sue and be sued in the same cases as the Dominion of India or the Provinces could before the Constitution, subject to any law made by Parliament or a State Legislature. Article 300(2): legal proceedings that were still running at the commencement of the Constitution against the Dominion of India continue against the Union of India. Proceedings against a Province or an Indian State continue against the corresponding State. Section 79, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: in a suit by or against the Government, the Union of India is named for the Central Government and the State for a State Government. Section 80, CPC: no suit may be filed against the Government, or against a public officer for an official act, until two months after a written notice. The notice goes to a Secretary of the Central Government (the General Manager for a railway), or to a Secretary or the Collector for a State. A suit for urgent relief may be filed with the leave of the court without notice. The court cannot grant any relief, interim or final, until the Government has had a reasonable chance to show cause. If the court finds that no urgent relief is needed, it returns the plaint to be filed again after notice (section 80(2)). A suit is not dismissed merely for an error in the notice if the plaintiff and the cause can be identified. Section 86, CPC: a foreign State can be sued only with the consent of the Central Government, certified in writing by a Secretary. A person may sue a foreign State without consent as a tenant of immovable property held from it. Consent is given only if the foreign State has itself sued the person, trades within the local limits, is in possession of immovable property there and is to be sued with reference to it, or has waived the privilege. No decree can be executed against its property without consent. The same rules apply to some other persons (section 86(4)). They are a ruler of a foreign State, an Ambassador or Envoy , a High Commissioner of a Commonwealth country, and staff members whom the Central Government specifies. A ruler of a foreign State, an Ambassador or Envoy and a High Commissioner of a Commonwealth country cannot be arrested under the Code. Nor can staff or retinue whom the Central Government specifies (section 86(5)). Articles 21, 32 and 226: the Supreme Court and the High Courts award compensation for violation of fundamental rights. Article 361(1): the President or a Governor is not answerable to any court for exercising the powers and duties of the office. The second proviso keeps open the right to bring proceedings against the Government of India or of a State. Article 361(4): no civil proceedings can be started during the term of office against the President or a Governor for a personal act until two months after written notice.

## Meaning and Definition
Sovereign immunity is the rule that the State cannot be sued in its own courts for torts committed in the exercise of sovereign power. It comes from the English maxim rex non potest peccare (the King can do no wrong). The Crown could not be sued in tort without its consent.

In England, the Crown Proceedings Act, 1947 changed this position. India has no equivalent Act. Parliament has not made a law under Article 300, so courts decide each case by applying the pre-1950 position and the Constitution.

The test that courts used is a distinction between two kinds of State function.

Sovereign functions. Acts that only a sovereign, or a person to whom the sovereign delegates power, can lawfully do. The cases name defence of the country and administration of justice (Chandrima Das). They also name arrest, search and seizure under statute (Kasturi Lal). Non-sovereign functions. Acts that a private person could also do, such as public transport, industry, state trading and running the Railways. Chandrima Das adds that a welfare State also works in education and in commercial, social, economic and political fields, and these are not sovereign.

## Key Points to Remember
- Article 300 fixes the extent of liability by pointing back to the pre-1950 position. It gives no rule of its own. The State is liable for non-sovereign functions (Vidyawati) and was not liable for sovereign ones (Kasturi Lal). P. and O. Steam Navigation Co. (1861) began the sovereign and non-sovereign test. Sovereign immunity is not a defence against public law compensation under Articles 32 and 226. Foreign State: suit only with the Central Government's consent (section 86 CPC). Act of state, as the Law Commission used the term: an act of the sovereign power against another sovereign power or its subjects, done in pursuance of sovereign rights. The Law Commission (1956) said the sovereign and non-sovereign distinction should no longer decide the State's liability. The 1967 Bill did not become law.
