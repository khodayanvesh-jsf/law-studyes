# 1. General Defences to an action in Torts
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/general-defences-to-an-action-in-torts/

## In One Minute
A claimant may prove a wrongful act and legal damage and still lose. The defendant may plead a general defence. These are grounds on which a defendant tries to escape or reduce liability. The main ones are volenti non fit injuria (consent to risk), plaintiff the wrongdoer, inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, necessity and statutory authority. Learn how consent to a risk works and where it fails, and learn the proof rule for inevitable accident.

## Legal Provision
- No Act lists the general defences. They come from decided cases. Sections 25 to 33, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023: consent to harm, acts done in good faith for a person's benefit, acts done under threat and trivial harm. They show how the criminal law treats consent. Sections 34 to 44, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023: the right of private defence of body and property, with its limits. Sections 3, 6, 22 and 23, Limitation Act, 1963, and Part VII of its Schedule: the time bar on a suit, and the periods for torts. Section 164(2), Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: in a claim for the fixed compensation, the claimant need not plead or prove a wrongful act, neglect or default. Rylands v. Fletcher (1868): Blackburn J named the claimant's default, vis major and act of God as possible excuses.

## Meaning and Definition
A general defence is a ground that the defendant pleads and proves to escape liability, in whole or in part, even though the claimant has proved the elements of a tort .

In Pushpabai Purshottam Udeshi (1977), the Supreme Court put the burden on the party pleading it. The defences fall in three groups.

Those based on the claimant's own act: volenti non fit injuria, plaintiff the wrongdoer, and contributory negligence. Those based on events no one could control: inevitable accident and act of God. Those based on the defendant's justified act: private defence, necessity and statutory authority.

## Key Points to Remember
- Inevitable accident must be proved by the party who pleads it (Pushpabai). Volenti: consent to a risk must be real. Fear or a misunderstanding of facts defeats it. Inevitable accident: the cause could not be avoided by ordinary care and caution. The party pleading it must prove it. Act of God: an excuse that the Rylands case left open. It is no defence to absolute liability. Private defence: never more harm than necessary (section 37(2)). Death may be caused only in the cases in sections 38 and 41. Necessity: section 30 of the Sanhita protects good faith acts for another's benefit, as a criminal-law analogy. Limitation ends a claim even if not pleaded (section 3).
