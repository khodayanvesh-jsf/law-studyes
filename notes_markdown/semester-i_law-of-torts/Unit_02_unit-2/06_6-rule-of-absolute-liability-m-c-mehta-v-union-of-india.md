# 6. Rule of Absolute Liability (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India)
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 2:** Unit 2
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/rule-of-absolute-liability/

## In One Minute
Absolute liability is the Indian rule for hazardous industry. An enterprise that carries on a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity must compensate everyone harmed by it. No defence is allowed: not act of God, not the act of a stranger, not statutory authority. The Supreme Court created the rule in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (decided December 1986, reported (1987) 1 SCC 395), after an oleum gas leak in Delhi. It also held that compensation must match the size and capacity of the enterprise. In exams, it is a long answer, often compared with Rylands v. Fletcher.

## Legal Provision
- Article 21, Constitution of India: the right to life is the foundation. Article 32: the Supreme Court can issue directions and, as in this case, decide compensation claims in a writ petition . Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, sections 3 and 8: the owner must pay relief for an accident with a hazardous substance, and the claimant need not prove a wrongful act. This relief is in addition to other claims. A workman is excluded. National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, sections 17 and 20: in case of an accident the Tribunal applies the principle of no fault (section 17(3)). The Act does not use the words "absolute liability". It tells the Tribunal to apply sustainable development, the precautionary principle and polluter pays (section 20).

## Meaning and Definition
Absolute liability is liability for harm caused by a hazardous activity, with no defences. The enterprise cannot say that it took all care, or that a stranger or nature caused the harm.

The Supreme Court laid down the rule in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987). It held that an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community to ensure that no harm results. If harm results, the enterprise is absolutely liable to compensate all those affected.

The facts of M.C. Mehta v. Union of India

M.C. Mehta had already filed a petition under Article 32 asking for the closure of the units of Shriram as hazardous. Relocation of the units became the remaining question. While it was before the Court, oleum gas escaped from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilizer Industries ("Shriram") in Delhi on 4 and 6 December 1985. The Delhi Legal Aid and Advice Board and the Delhi Bar Association then applied for compensation for the persons harmed.

A bench of three judges had earlier allowed Shriram to restart its plants on conditions. Because the applications raised questions on Articles 21 and 32, the case was referred to a Constitution Bench of five judges. Bhagwati CJ gave the judgment of the Court.

## Key Points to Remember
- Absolute liability applies to hazardous or inherently dangerous activity. No defence is available: not act of God, act of a stranger, statutory authority or the claimant's default. The duty to the community is non-delegable. Compensation matches the size and capacity of the enterprise. The Court's test is harm from an accident in the operation of the activity. It does not mention escape from land as a condition. The Court left open whether a private company like Shriram is an authority under Article 12. In the Bichhri case (1996) the Court called the law in the Oleum Gas Leak case "the law governing this case". Source: M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987), an Indian rule wider than Rylands v. Fletcher.
