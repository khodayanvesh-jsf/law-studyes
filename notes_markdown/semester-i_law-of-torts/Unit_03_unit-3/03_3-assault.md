# 3. Assault
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 3:** Unit 3
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/assault/

## In One Minute
Assault is a gesture or preparation that makes a person present expect that criminal force is about to be used on them. The blow need not land: in Stephens v. Myers the defendant was stopped before any blow, and the jury was asked whether his advance was an assault. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 defines it in section 130. The case of Stephens v. Myers (1830) shows how the courts treated a threat that was stopped before the blow. In exams, this topic is asked as a question to define assault and state its essentials, and as a problem on words, gestures and threats that cannot be carried out.

## Legal Provision
- Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, section 130 defines assault for the criminal law. The civil claim in this note rests on Stephens v. Myers (1830). Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, section 130: a person commits assault who makes any gesture , or any preparation , intending or knowing it to be likely that a person present will apprehend that the maker is about to use criminal force on them. Section 130, Explanation: mere words do not amount to an assault. But the words a person uses may give the gesture or preparation a meaning that makes it an assault. Section 130, illustrations: (a) A shakes his fist at Z, intending or knowing it likely that Z will believe A is about to strike. This is an assault. (b) A begins to unloose the muzzle of a ferocious dog, intending or knowing it likely that Z will believe A is about to make the dog attack Z. This is an assault. (c) A takes up a stick, saying to Z, "I will give you a beating". The words alone could not be an assault, and the gesture alone might not be. The gesture explained by the words may be an assault. Section 129: criminal force means intentionally using force on a person, without that person's consent , in order to commit an offence, or intending or knowing it likely that the force will cause injury, fear or annoyance . Section 131: assault or criminal force, other than on grave and sudden provocation given by that person, is punished with imprisonment of either description up to three months, or fine up to one thousand rupees, or both. Section 131, Explanation 1: provocation does not reduce the punishment in three cases. The offender sought or voluntarily provoked it as an excuse for the offence. It was given by something done in obedience to the law, or by a public servant in the lawful exercise of their powers. It was given by something done in the lawful exercise of the right of private defence. Section 131, Explanation 2: whether the provocation was grave and sudden enough is a question of fact. Section 136: assault or criminal force on grave and sudden provocation given by the victim: simple imprisonment up to one month, or fine up to one thousand rupees, or both. It is subject to the same Explanation as section 131. Other aggravated forms: Section 132: assault or criminal force on a public servant in the execution of duty, to prevent or deter the officer from duty, or because of anything the officer did or tried to do in the lawful discharge of duty. Up to two years, or fine, or both. Section 133: assault or criminal force intending to dishonour the person, other than on grave and sudden provocation. Up to two years, or fine, or both. Section 134: assault or criminal force in attempting to commit theft of property the person is wearing or carrying. Up to two years, or fine, or both. Section 135: assault or criminal force in attempting wrongfully to confine a person. Up to one year, or fine up to five thousand rupees, or both. Limitation: Article 113 of the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963 allows three years from when the right to sue accrues for any suit with no period of limitation provided elsewhere in the Schedule.

## Meaning and Definition
Assault is a gesture or preparation by the defendant that makes a person present expect that the defendant is about to use criminal force on them (section 130). The test is the expectation of force, not a blow. Actual force without consent is criminal force under section 129.

Section 130 has three elements: a gesture or preparation, a person present who is made to expect criminal force, and the maker's intention or knowledge that this is likely. Section 130 and its illustrations do not mention a further requirement of means. That requirement comes from the civil case: Tindal C.J. in Stephens v. Myers told the jury there must be the means of carrying the threat into effect.

Criminal force is itself defined in section 129. Its illustrations show how wide force is: unfastening a boat's moorings, lashing a rider's horses, pushing a person in the street, throwing a stone, pulling up a woman's veil, pouring boiling water into a bath, and inciting a dog to spring on a person. Section 130 applies where a gesture or preparation makes a person expect such force.

## Key Points to Remember
- Assault is making a person present expect criminal force (section 130). The blow need not land. Section 130 elements: gesture or preparation, apprehension, intention or knowledge. Means of carrying out the threat: the civil test in Stephens v. Myers. Mere words are not assault, but words can give a gesture its meaning (section 130, Explanation). Stephens v. Myers (1830): Tindal C.J. directed the jury that an advance that would reach the claimant within a second or two is an assault in law. Verdict for the claimant. Punishment: section 131 (three months, or fine up to one thousand rupees), section 136 (one month, on grave provocation), sections 132 to 135 (aggravated forms).
