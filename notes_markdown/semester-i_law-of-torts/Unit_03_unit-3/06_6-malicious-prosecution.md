# 6. Malicious Prosecution
**Subject:** Law of Torts (Semester I) | **Unit 3:** Unit 3
**Source:** https://nyayasaral.com/subjects/law-of-torts/malicious-prosecution/

## In One Minute
Malicious prosecution is the tort of starting a criminal case against a person from a wrong motive and without probable cause, when the case ends in that person's favour. The Supreme Court collected the law on malice and on this tort in West Bengal State Electricity Board v. Dilip Kumar Ray (2006). The usual list of elements is a proceeding started by the defendant, a favourable end, no reasonable and probable cause, malice and damage. The judgment itself states two essentials: no probable cause and a favourable end. The sources used here are two Sanhitas, the Limitation Act, the old Code of Criminal Procedure and that one judgment.

## Legal Provision
- None of the statutes used here defines this tort. They deal with the criminal court's power to award compensation, a criminal offence of false charge, and the time limit for a civil suit. Section 273(1), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (earlier section 250, Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973): in a case started on a complaint or on information to the police or a Magistrate, an accused is tried for an offence triable by a Magistrate. If the Magistrate discharges or acquits and thinks there was no reasonable ground for the accusation, he may call on the complainant to show cause why he should not pay compensation to the accused. Section 273(2) and (3): the Magistrate records and considers the cause shown. If satisfied that there was no reasonable ground, he may, for reasons recorded, order compensation up to the fine he can impose. He may order simple imprisonment up to thirty days in default . Section 273(5): the order does not free the complainant from civil or criminal liability for the complaint. Any amount paid is taken into account in awarding compensation in a later civil suit on the same matter. Section 273(6) and (7): a complainant ordered by a second class Magistrate to pay more than two thousand rupees may appeal. Compensation is not paid before the appeal period ends, or before the appeal is decided. Where no appeal lies, it is not paid for one month. Section 273(8): the section applies to summons-cases and warrant-cases. Section 399 (earlier section 358 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973): if a person causes a police officer to arrest another, and the Magistrate finds no sufficient ground, the Magistrate may award up to one thousand rupees for loss of time and expenses. The earlier Code allowed one hundred rupees. Unpaid compensation can be recovered like a fine, with up to thirty days of simple imprisonment. Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, section 248: whoever, with intent to cause injury, starts or causes to be started a criminal proceeding against a person, or falsely charges a person with an offence, knowing there is no just or lawful ground, commits an offence. The punishment is imprisonment up to five years, or fine up to two lakh rupees, or both. If the false charge is of an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment or ten years or more, the term can go up to ten years, with fine. Limitation Act, 1963, Article 74: a suit for compensation for a malicious prosecution must be filed within one year. The period runs from when the plaintiff is acquitted (found not guilty) or the prosecution is otherwise terminated.

## Meaning and Definition
Malicious prosecution is the use of criminal process against a person from a wrong motive and without probable cause. The Dilip Kumar Ray judgment records several definitions. It says it is "a judicial proceeding instituted by one person against another, from wrongful or improper motive and without probable cause to sustain it".

Another definition in the judgment is "a prosecution begun in malice, without probable cause to believe that it can succeed and which finally ends in failure". The judgment adds that the term "imports a causeless as well as an ill-intended prosecution".

The judgment also quotes Black's Law Dictionary. Once a wrongful prosecution has ended in the defendant's favour, he or she may sue for tort damages. The same dictionary says the proceeding may be criminal or civil.

## Key Points to Remember
- Usual elements: proceeding started by the defendant, favourable end, no reasonable and probable cause, malice, damage. Malice is an improper or indirect motive, not only spite. If there was reasonable or probable cause, no amount of malice makes the defendant liable. The onus of proving malice and want of cause is on the plaintiff. Abuse of process misuses process for another purpose. Malicious prosecution causes process to be issued maliciously. Time limit: one year from the acquittal (Article 74).
